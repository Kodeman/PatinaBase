Our reading of the business

Patina belongs at the moment
a studio learns to share the work.

The studio

A growing practice bringing in its first hands. Someone new needs to understand the job without asking the owner to explain it again.

The client

The studio’s client, looking at one agreed direction. Every choice should show what is being decided and what happens next.

The maker

The studio’s vendor, receiving the right specification and a clear release. The history of a piece stays with the job.

The subscription earns its place in the daily work. Furniture margin follows when a designer-led selection becomes a well-carried order.

A project book, always open

Folio

The designer’s point of view becomes the organizing principle.

Folio / Designer preview
A visual project book with a reading index.Select the preview to explore.
Quiet / expressive / personal

The idea

A room photograph and a short letter set the direction. Decisions sit in the story of the house, where clients can understand them.

Strongest for

Presenting the studio’s judgment. A new colleague can read into the project; a client feels the care behind a recommendation.

The tradeoff

Less information at a glance. A busy buying day needs a concise reading index and excellent prioritization.

The room is the working surface

Workroom

Spatial thinking connects inspiration to a precise specification.

Workroom / Designer preview
A room composition with a focused specification inspector.Select the preview to explore.
Visual / precise / hands-on

The idea

Start with the room and select a piece to work on it. Materials, dimensions, and the next decision stay beside the composition.

Strongest for

Sourcing and specifying. The team sees why a piece belongs in the room while keeping the details close enough to act on.

The tradeoff

The canvas asks more of small screens and keyboard navigation. It needs a strong linear reading mode as work grows.

Explore Workroom

In the prototype

Select the chair or a material, inspect its details, and make a sample finish choice in the client view.

For the founders: should Patina feel like an instrument designers use with their eyes and hands?

One clear agreement at a time

Common Ground

The shared understanding of the job becomes the interface.

Common Ground / Designer preview
A project spine connecting what is agreed to what comes next.Select the preview to explore.
Human / clear / reassuring

The idea

Begin with what everyone has agreed. Give the next decision a clear home, with the studio’s working notes kept distinct from the client’s view.

Strongest for

Delegating and coordinating. A colleague can see what changed, who is needed, and which information is ready to share.

The tradeoff

Less expressive room exploration. Without a generous visual layer, decisions could feel like administration.

Explore Common Ground

In the prototype

Compare the shared and studio-only information, record a sample decision, and inspect the maker handoff.

For the founders: should Patina’s defining feeling be “we all know where this stands”?

Our discussion starter

Common Ground most directly serves the first-hire moment. Folio gives the strongest expression of the studio’s care. Workroom offers the boldest change in how design work happens.

This is a design hypothesis, not a usability finding. The founder’s preference should lead to a test with a real job.

After choosing: prototype one complete path—room direction, client decision, maker-ready brief—and have Leah hand it to a colleague without explaining it.

About this study

Prepared for Patina’s founders, October 20, 2026. Three independent design-agent explorations, assembled into one review. The brief comes from Patina’s VISION, its client-page ruling (V8), buying decisions (V14), and draft-first communication rule (V22), plus the brand voice canon.

All prototype names, specifications, dates, and workflow events are fictional. Middle West Studio is the real business context; this is not a real client record. Role switching demonstrates proposed visibility, not production authorization. No prototype sends a message or places an order.

Photographs illustrate material and atmosphere. The photographed chair is not a Northline Workshop product; Northline is a fictional maker.
