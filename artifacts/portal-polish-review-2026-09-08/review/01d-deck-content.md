# Adversarial review — the deck's content and design

**Target:** `deck/index.html` ("Paper, Polished", 18 sheets) and its 36 viewport
renders plus 3 dark plates in `shots/deck/`.
**Reviewer:** fresh context. I did not build this and took nothing from the
builder's report on trust.
**Method:** read `briefing/panel-brief-common.md`, `briefing/proposal-digest.md`,
`synthesis.md`, all seven `panel/*.md`, `specimens/SPEC.md` §A, then the deck
HTML in full (base64 stripped) and every render at 1440 and 390 plus the three
dark plates. Fifteen `file:line` citations opened in the repo. Six contrast
ratios and one SVG type size recomputed from the hex values. The outside
proposal's source (`/private/tmp/patina-design-proposal-2026-09-07-kul503/index.html`)
grepped for the name and quote claims. Two crops of
`shots/current/client-local-dev-desktop.png` enlarged to settle two money figures.

**Headline.** The deck is honest, well-argued and mostly well-typeset, and its
evidence holds up under recomputation better than any deck I have checked: the
three published contrast ratios, the 1.75:1 resting score, the 4.20:1 oak, the
3.9px footprint label and fourteen of fifteen `file:line` cites all verify
exactly. Three things stop it from being finished. First, **it prints a money
figure that does not exist** — "$7,040.00 paid" — on the slide arguing that money
must be trustworthy, directly under a plate that says "$0 paid". Second, **the
decision slide hides its own decision**: sheet 17 shows 6 of 18 rows at 1440 and
every Decline row sits behind a silent inner scroll. Third, **the deck breaks
three of the five rules it publishes** — the 15px money floor (sheet 11), the
what/whose/**when** caption (every plate), and the 24px module (its own CSS) —
which is precisely the charge it lays against the proposal on sheet 7.

There is no one-line verdict anywhere in eighteen sheets.
