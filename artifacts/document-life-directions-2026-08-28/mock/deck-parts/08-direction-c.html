Direction C — The Dark Desk

Canon D4 says depth is value contrast, and the product spends it almost nowhere in the working shell: the five charcoal grounds the contrast test names are the mobile bar, the mobile sheets, the sub-1180 log strip and the two client-preview banners — so the only charcoal a designer meets above 1180px is a preview of what the client sees.

C takes D4's own mechanism to its conclusion. The chrome goes charcoal and the paper stays paper: the desk is a dark desk with one lit sheet on it, the spine and margin are the desk showing beside the document, and the drawer is a well cut one step deeper still. One warm clay rule marks every edge where the sheet meets the desk. Nothing on the sheet itself changes.

The scope of the inversion. Four surfaces go charcoal: the Studio Drawer, the DocSpine, the MarginRail, and the desk route's ground. Library, People and the ledger sheets keep paper and receive only the dark drawer along the bottom edge, because the drawer is the one piece of chrome that crosses every route.

What stays identical

Every route, component, act, label and piece of information architecture. The greeting, the three acts and their sub-labels, both whisper notes, the roster eyebrow and its stage groups in order, one line per job and never a card, the studio index, the drawer's five doorways. The document keeps its spine with the seven marks, the running index, the in-hand block, its letterhead, red-letter zone, region heads and ledgers, its FF&E lines by room, the margin's notes and chips. C changes which ground each is printed on and nothing else — what the sheet gains is the planks, which A and B adopt identically.

The risk taken

The desk route inverts too. Charcoal chrome around a lit document is the easy half; the hard half is that the desk — the screen a designer opens first every morning — becomes a dark room with one sheet in it. That is C's whole argument, and the reason C can be rejected in one glance. If the desk route is refused and only the document's chrome inverts, C survives as a document treatment: the first figure below is the one to rule on.

Why the registers never meet. No colour clears 4.5:1 on both #FCFAF6 and #2C2926: from the two grounds' relative luminances, the cap on paper is 0.1738 and the floor on charcoal is 0.2768. So a token belongs to one register or the other, and which one applies is decided by the ground it prints on, never by the component.

Findings addressed

The planks close the reading floor, the muted inks, the rule weights, hover, the state fill and the fourth ground in all three lanes. What C closes alone:

Canon check

  • D4, five lint selectors: none tripped, and C is the strongest argument against ever needing a shadow — on a dark ground twelve stops of value lift a sheet without one. C argues against the elevation amendment; the question itself is put to the team later in this deck.
  • D1: untouched. The chrome changes colour; it does not gain a surface, a tab or a panel.
  • Typography-first: the type is untouched; what C adds is value, the mechanism D4 itself names.
  • Never a card: the roster stays lines on a sheet, and the sheet is the same object the document route already paints — one rule for an edge, no radius, not a container around each row.
  • The contrast test breaks, twice, and the fix is a required extension. Its parser is last-wins over the whole file and asserts every -ink-suffixed token against the light grounds, so a dark-register token named that way fails hard; and the scoped-override alternative is worse, because it overwrites the real token's parsed value. C must ship a dark register with no -ink suffix and a light/dark split in the parser. Separately, the base-pigment guard's exemption list goes from five files to eleven — a guard being widened, not extended.
  • The planks arrive hardcoded charcoal, and C is the lane that has to notice. The three rule weights and the state fill are values, not roles: on C's chrome they measure 1.000 to 1.159, and the fill is a 10.408 near-white blob on the desk. Every plank token needs a dark twin, not only every ink. The twins are declared in the planks and priced here.

Keeps · Refuses · Costs

Keeps
The paper, at the shipped #FCFAF6. The shipped pigments, at their shipped values. Every route, act, label and region in place and in order.
Refuses
No type-scale change beyond the planks. No tinted paper, no darkened paper, no imagery, no texture. No night mode, no toggle, no theme switch — C is one appearance, not two. It argues against the amendment rather than asking for it.
Scope
Four surfaces, named above. The desk ground is painted from a desk-route token, not --bg-primary, which the route shell paints under every document route. Turning Library, People and the ledgers dark later is a second piece of work, not a token flip: People alone renders 78 pearl border-sides and 20 white card backgrounds tuned for a cream ground, and none of the three is drawn here. Price it as three to four more days and three rooms to draw. Keeping those rooms on paper is not keeping them unchanged: the drawer crosses every route, and it goes from white to #201D1B — a 16.077:1 charcoal well along the bottom edge of a cream room, on exactly the routes this paragraph says are untouched, and no figure in this deck draws it. It is the right trade and it is the price of the argument that the drawer crosses every route; the missing artifact is one Library crop.
Costs
Four to five days of C's own work, one day for the contrast-test parser and the exemption decision, plus the planks. The register conversion is not an ink problem: the dark twins for the three rule weights and the state fill are part of the same sweep through the same chrome components, and they are inside the four to five days. The risk is in the tail: the dark register has to be complete, or one unconverted muted ink inside the spine is a 1.505:1 line nobody can read, and one unconverted plank rule is a 1.000:1 line nobody can see — both failures are silent, not loud.
