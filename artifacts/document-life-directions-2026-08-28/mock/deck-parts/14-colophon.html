How this was made, and what it rests on

Method, in order

  1. Scaffold and kit port. The program folder, the mock kit and its four I151 -ink tokens, the build and QA scripts ported from two earlier deck programs.
  2. Fresh captures. Twenty-two shots at 1440 and 390, taken 2026-08-28 against the local seed, signed in as designer Leah Hartwell. Twenty-two of twenty-two exist; twenty-one verified as the intended surface, one a documented, source-confirmed unreachable state.
  3. A DOM measurement script. Every visible element's computed style, read off the rendered page at /desk, /doc/<Chen Residence>, /library and /people, contrast math ported verbatim from contrast.test.ts.
  4. One audit seat. A simulated designer's eye — a senior interior designer at a Midwest studio, stated plainly wherever it speaks — walked every capture against the measurer's numbers and wrote the findings.
  5. Priors harvest. Verbatim quotes with file and line from four earlier reviews of the same surface, folded in beside the fresh findings.
  6. One author, three lanes. A single context wrote Directions A, B and C, the shared planks and the amendment together, for coherence across lanes rather than three separate voices.
  7. An adversarial critic. A fresh context, never the author, found thirty-nine defects (D01–D39) against the repo, the fresh captures and recomputed WCAG arithmetic.
  8. v2. The same author answered every defect — fix, accept, or drop with a stated reason — against the same critic's rubric.
  9. A second critique, same critic. Thirty-five of thirty-nine resolved, four partly, zero unresolved, zero disputed — and ten new defects surfaced by the revision itself (D40–D49), mostly residue of retunes that fixed one number and left its neighbor stale.
  10. A v3 number pass. Where D40–D49 asked for a printed figure to be corrected, the direction docs were updated once more; this deck quotes whatever value stood in the source files at the moment each part was written.
  11. Deck parts, build gates, QA, fact-check. Fourteen sections, each under its own character budget, each F-id and D-id checked against the research folder; a build that fails on a stray shadow, an unresolved marker, or a disallowed host; a rendered QA pass at both widths and both themes.

What this deck does not claim

  • No user research was run. Every finding, every quote and every “blends” comes from one simulated seat and the measurements beside it, never from a designer's own session.
  • The designer's voice throughout this deck is a simulated seat — a senior interior designer at a Midwest studio, stated plainly wherever it speaks — not a real practitioner walked through the product.
  • Every capture in this deck is the local seed, not a production account. StatusChip never rendered on this data: plan_sheets and every proposal_items row with a product_id are both empty locally, and the one shot attempted at it is a documented failure, not a verified render. The install-stage shot required lifting the project's state by RPC, not a naturally occurring row.
  • Direction B's imagery is scoped for the mock, not for the product: on the local seed, zero of six FF&E lines carry a product_id, so B's headline material move renders zero thumbnails here, and the two thumbnails in its mock are stood in to show the treatment.
  • The six-axis scorecard is one critic's, checked twice, never a vote of more than one and never averaged into a single number.
  • Nothing in this program changed the product. No migration was written, no component was edited, no route moved — every mock is CSS on a specimen, built and inspected outside apps/designer-portal.
  • The D4 amendment is a question put to the team, not a proposal this deck argues for. No mockup here illustrates it, and no direction depends on its answer either way.
