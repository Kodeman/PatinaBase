# Memo — the practicing designer's lens

Leah's seat. Principal, three people, residential, just hired my first assistant. Not a software person. I run jobs out
of my head, a legal pad, and my phone. Here's how those cards read to me.

## 1. Findings on the screenshot's card

**"Waiting on Kody for 30 days" — the counter shames, and it's usually wrong. (high)**
I never think in counters. I think "the Halvorsens have been sitting on it since Labor Day." A date I can place against
my own week is information; a number that grows every morning is a scold — and it doesn't know I spoke to them Tuesday
and they asked for two weeks. Worse, my new assistant reads it as a verdict and starts apologizing to clients who
aren't upset. Give me the date and the last real contact.

**"Proposal pending 44 days" — "pending" is bank language. (high)**
What I need: did they open it, when, and have they said anything. "Sent 2 Sep · opened three times, last Sunday · no
signature" tells me to call. "Pending 44 days" tells me to feel bad.

**"Sample, chosen 43 days" / "Living room 4, chosen 3 days" — I can't read these. (high)**
A sample chosen 43 days ago isn't a status, it's trivia. "Living room 4" is a database row wearing a hat. If a line
can't be said aloud to my assistant without translating, it doesn't belong.

**"1 · Client: Kody" — I'd never say "Client:". (high)**
Nobody says "client colon." We say "the Halvorsens," "Nora," "the lake house." Label-colon-value is how a spreadsheet
talks. The bare "1 ·" counts something I didn't ask about.

**"Presented / Approved / In Production / Installed" — that's a *piece's* life, not a *house's*. (high)**
A sofa is presented, approved, in production, installed. A house is never at one of those: on Sonnenberg six pieces are
in production, three are installed, one's stuck at the upholsterer, and I still owe Nora two bedrooms. A house-level
strip has to pick one, and whichever it picks is a lie. A house has an arc — brief, discovery, direction, proposal,
ordering, install, care — and I only need to know which room of it we're standing in. (A strip could work on a single
*piece* card; that isn't this. med-high)

**Fifteen cards shouting equally. (high)** On a normal Tuesday one job is on fire and fourteen are fine. The screen
should look like that.

## 2. What goes on the card

1. **The name I say out loud** — "Halvorsen townhouse," "Cedar Lane Study." Serif, ~20px, first thing, wrapping if
   long. Never cut off: "Reinhardt lake ho…" is unacceptable.
2. **Where we are, in my words** — small mono meta: "Direction · Concept," "Proposal out," "Ordering," "Install week."
3. **The one true sentence** — what's stuck, whose court, with a date. Body 14, darkest thing after the name, two lines
   max. Grammar: "With the Halvorsens since 2 Sep — opened three times, last Sunday, no signature." · "Nora replied
   last night — two rooms waiting on your mark-up." · "Install slipped 4 Sep — nothing rescheduled." · "$17,500 past
   due since 12 Aug; one piece blocked at the workroom." Dates not counters, names not roles.
4. **Whose pen it is** — carried by the sentence, not a badge. "Waiting on them" and "waiting on you" are different
   mornings; I sort my day by that difference.
5. **One thing to do** — "Send a reminder," "Review the decisions," "Open the schedule." One. Offer three and I do none.

**Three things I'd be annoyed to see:** day counters and anything that ticks (including "you last touched this 12 days
ago" — I hired an assistant, not a hall monitor); percentages and progress bars (a house is not 62% complete); count
soup — "4 rooms · 11 items · 2 pending." I don't manage counts, I manage the one that's wrong.

**Quiet.** Osterberg is in care and nothing is wrong. That card reads as a finished sentence, not an empty form: name,
"Care · nothing needs you," done. No mark, no chip, no em-dashes standing in for missing data, no greyed slots implying
something failed to load. Quiet cards should be *shorter and lighter* than busy ones — a calm house should look calm,
not broken.

**Names.** Both, never both at once by default. If the house is the family — most residential work — the family name
*is* the house name and there's no second line. If the house has its own name, that leads and the person appears only
inside the sentence when a person is involved ("Nora replied last night"). New leads get a full name once — I haven't
earned the first name yet. No "Client:" label anywhere.

**Phone vs desk.** Kitchen counter, 6:40, coffee in the other hand: three cards, not fifteen — the ones that need me
today, readable at arm's length, act big enough to hit with a thumb without opening the job. Everything quiet collapses
to a line I scroll past. At the desk: all sixteen, quiet ones visibly quiet, loud ones top-left, whole studio swept in
eight seconds before my assistant walks in.

## 3. Shape

A sheet of paper on the desk, not a tile: a shade lighter than the ground so it reads as something you could pick up,
hairline edge, corners barely eased, **no shadow** — depth is the value step and the real edge. 24px air. Top-left, a
7px mark: filled terracotta = late and it's ours; filled clay = waiting on someone else; hairline ring = nothing wrong.
Three states, not one per stage. Then name / stage line / sentence, a hairline rule, the act bottom-right in small caps
with a scored underline.

Height follows the sentence — quiet cards are genuinely shorter and the grid should let them be. Hover: paper lifts one
value step, name's underline darkens. Focus: 2px ring. Press: the paper goes one step *down*, like pressing a sheet
flat. At 15 jobs, three columns, loud first. At 45, still three columns, but the quiet tail compresses to one-line
entries — a card that says nothing isn't worth a card's worth of desk. At 390px, one column: the loud three
full-height, the rest as lines, act stays a 44px target.

## 4. Contract

Honors: one focused screen, no tabs, no shadows, no red/green, no badges, no counts, nothing rewarding me for coming
back. Departs, knowingly, on "never a card." The honest reason a card can beat a line: it holds **one sentence and one
act with air around them**, which makes a job feel like a house rather than a row. Worth it only while the card stays a
sheet of paper. The moment it grows a second act, a chip row, or a metric, the roster was right and we go back.

## 5. The one thing for Kody to rule

**Do quiet houses get cards at all?** Past ten quiet jobs, cards for houses where nothing is happening turn my desk
into wallpaper and bury the one that matters. My instinct: cards for what needs a hand today, a plain list underneath
for everything at rest — but that makes the Desk two things, not one grid, so it should be ruled, not assumed.
(High confidence fifteen equal cards is wrong; medium on which fix.)

## The ideal card, plainly

A warm sheet of paper, hairline edge, no shadow. Top-left a 7-pixel mark: clay if someone else is holding it,
terracotta if it's late and ours, an empty ring if nothing's wrong. The house's name in the serif face — the name the
designer says out loud — wrapping if long. Under it, quietly, where we are in the arc. Then one true sentence about
this house today: what happened, who it's waiting on, and the date. If nothing is wrong the sentence is "Nothing needs
you" and the card ends there, shorter and lighter than its neighbours. If something is wrong, a hairline rule and one
act, bottom-right, thumb-sized. Tap anywhere else and it opens the house.
