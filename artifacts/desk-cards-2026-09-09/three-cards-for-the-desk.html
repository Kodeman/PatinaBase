Three cards for the Desk

Kody asked for project cards on the Desk. The screenshot he sent showed fifteen equal tiles: a name, a sub-line reading “1 · Client: Kody”, a day counter, and on some a four-step strip from Presented to Installed. That grid is not what runs on prod. The live Desk is a one-line-per-job roster, and on 26 August we wrote the rule down: one line per job; never a card.

He is asking for cards again, so the rule is revisited in the open rather than quietly contradicted. Five lenses read the screenshot against the data model and the design contract. Three cards came out of it, live below in HTML on one fictional studio.

Section two

What we would cut, and why

Five lines on the screenshot’s card do not survive. Each is replaced by something the data can already say honestly.

Was 1 · Client: Kody

Is Kody Kochaver

The leading ordinal counts nothing a designer asked about, and nobody says “client colon” out loud. Position carries the label. Print the person bare.

Was Waiting on Kody for 30 days

Is With Kody since 2 Sep

A number that only grows is an accusation, and it does not know they asked for two weeks on Tuesday. A date can be held against this week; a counter can only be felt bad about.

Was Presented / Approved / In Production / Installed

Is Direction

That is a piece’s life, not a house’s. On Sonnenberg six pieces are in production, three installed, one stuck at the upholsterer — a four-step rail must pick one, and whichever it picks is a lie. A house stands in one room of the arc, brief through care.

Was 15 projects

Is Two things are overdue — Vandersteen, install, since 4 Sep; Sonnenberg residence, $17,500, since 12 Aug

A tally of the studio’s own volume is a scoreboard, and the studio surface is never optimized for engagement. The day’s line is a call, not a count.

Was Fifteen cards of equal weight

Is Five cards over eleven rows

On a normal Tuesday one job is on fire and fourteen are fine. The screen should look like that. A quiet house should read calm, not broken.

Section three

What every card carries

Six registers, fixed order, one type role each. The numbering is not decoration — the registers are an order, and it is the order the eye takes them in. One exploded card, pulled apart:

  1. 1

    Stage

    One word on the existing stage plate. DM Mono 11 uppercase, white label on the section pigment. Never a count beside it.

  2. 2

    Custody

    The word names the hand: Your pen · With Nora · With the maker · At rest. The 7px mark beside it grades the need — terracotta ink for urgent or overdue, clay ink for a quiet need, a faint ring for nothing needed. Color never carries custody; the word does.

  3. 3

    Name

    The name she says out loud. Playfair 500 at 20/1.3, oak-scored as the link, two lines reserved so neighbours share a baseline. It wraps; it never truncates.

  4. 4

    Person · phase

    Inter 14, muted, no label. Where the family name is the house name, this line is the phase alone. It disambiguates; it never drives the decision.

  5. 5

    The one true sentence

    Grammar: subject or figure — dated fact or required act. One em dash at most. Money takes the mono money step, the broken clause takes terracotta ink. Quiet reads Nothing needs your hand. and the card stops.

  6. 6

    The one act

    DM Mono 13 uppercase, oak-scored tertiary; two-score secondary where a reminder moves or money opens. 44px target, labelled with the house name so eleven cards do not all announce “Open”. Never two acts. A quiet card has none — the name is the act.

Depth is the value step between --paper-doc and --paper, plus a hairline — no shadow, no borrowed elevation token. Hover washes the stage pigment open from the pointer at 7%; focus is a 2px clay-ink ring at 2px offset; press thickens the act’s score to 2px. Nothing lifts.

Honors

Zero shadow, two radii, one act, one sentence, no counts and no progress rail. The quiet card is visibly shorter and lighter — the proof the Desk is telling the truth.

Departs

It is a card, and it re-ranks the studio every morning. Position becomes a claim: the top-left tile is Patina saying what to care about first, printed with its reason so a wrong rank is legible.

What it costs — at forty-five jobs, fifteen rows of three-column scanning and some four thousand pixels of desk, most of it spent on houses where nothing is wrong.

Honors

The smallest departure on the page: one line per job, with air and a grid. Plates kept, mark kept, nothing folded on first paint. It wins the one thing the roster could not do — two dates or two figures compared down the page.

Departs

A card only as a ledger entry is a card: an edge and a measure, not a tile. The value column is new architecture, and it forces one value per job where the truth is sometimes two. The value is the sentence’s own date or figure, so its meaning is read from the sentence beside it — a quiet job carries none.

What it costs — about 1.4× the roster’s scroll at forty-five jobs, and the value must be chosen by rule rather than judgement.

      Section six

      Rulings for Kody

      Five decisions, not a sequence. Nothing below should be settled in code.

      R1

      Do quiet jobs get cards at all?

      At sixteen jobs with one overdue, a full grid spends most of its area on houses where nothing is wrong — the engagement failure arrived at by geometry rather than intent. The information architect, the practicing designer and the interaction designer all say no. Card 3 encodes that; Card 1 softens it with a shorter, lighter tile; Card 2 gives a quiet job a band the height of a row.

      R2

      Does the card replace the roster, or become a third facet?

      A facet means two renderings of one model kept in step forever, and a view switcher, which is one step from the dashboard the vision refuses. The typographer says replace — one rendering, honestly amended. Replacing costs roughly 1.4× the scroll at forty-five jobs and formally rescinds the 26 August density rule.

      R3

      Does the Desk rank, or merely mark?

      Need-first order puts Patina’s judgement top-left every morning; stage-first is never wrong and never helpful. It costs more in a grid because position is the message — one stale due date puts a fine house top-left and the instrument loses credibility in a glance. The panel leans rank, with the reason printed on the card so a wrong rank is correctable rather than mysterious.

      R4

      The card edge.

      No token reaches 3:1 against paper — hairline is 1.20:1, strong is 1.30:1, and the second stock on the ground is 1.03:1 — and D4 forbids the shadow that would rescue it. Either amend “no new token” for one boundary grey near #8F8C88, used only as a card edge, or accept a decorative edge where the box groups and the text carries everything. The panel accepts the decorative edge; all three cards above are built on that.

      R5

      Amend the 26 August rule in writing.

      Whichever card ships, the recorded rule is contradicted. Amend it plainly — “one line per job in the roster; a job with a claim on the studio’s hand may take a card” — rather than leaving the record silently wrong. That sentence is also the whole design argument in one line.

      Found along the way

      Two live defects in the designer portal, independent of any card. The roster’s 7px mark is drawn in the material members of the pigment pairs — terracotta at 2.13:1, dusty blue at 2.64:1 — where the paper-ink members were intended. And every focus ring in the portal uses clay at 2.18:1 instead of the clay-ink the spec computes at 5.61:1: the right value was worked out and the wrong token wired. Both are one-token swaps.
