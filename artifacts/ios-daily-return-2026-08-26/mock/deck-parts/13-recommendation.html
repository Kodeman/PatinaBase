The three rulings

Ruling one · the home

Does Today become the Record card and the house rail?

Recommendation: yes, after the slice. It is what the homeowner and the designer judges paid for — her own rooms on the screen, every waiting thing dated on one card. The card is an additive mount, so it can ship behind a fail-closed flag evaluated once at launch and held for the session. The open conflict is between the home that was accepted last July and the tier-gated rail that is still orphaned in the code one mount away; that is a ruling, not a finding, and no one on this review is entitled to make it.

Ruling two · the shell

Does the app get a tab bar?

Recommendation: do not decide it from a deck. It is the most expensive and riskiest item in either direction — a navigation root swapped across 105 call sites, both roots carried for a release — and it is the one the alignment deck already deferred until real use existed. Ship the labelled Studio control in the slice, walk it, then rule. If the answer is yes, it goes behind the same flag with both roots kept. The door complaint is the highest-consensus one in the program, and the door can be fixed without answering the shell.

F11 F126 F134 F121

Ruling three · the money

Can a client who already has a designer buy a piece without her?

Recommendation: not at first. Both directions agree on the parts that are free today — attribution written on the order before money moves, Buy gated on a piece that is genuinely buyable, a responsibility paragraph with a reachable human, and a tax ruling that is Kody’s. Take A’s pre-emption as the default until the designer learns about the order on a device, then B’s “Buy it myself” with its disclosure. Building direct orders ahead of the ratified reviews and scope-change items is a conscious reversal of the backlog order; both directions name it, and Kody rules it.
