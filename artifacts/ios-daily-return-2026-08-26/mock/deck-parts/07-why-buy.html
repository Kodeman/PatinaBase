Why anyone would buy here

The money rail is built, armed, and pointed at a button nobody drew. What is missing is not the payment. It is everything a person needs to know before they make one.

Every browse path in the app ends in the same place. The piece screen’s whole commercial vocabulary is one primary button reading “Add to Room”, which once tapped reads “Saved ✓”, with a back chevron, a ?, share and a heart above it. No cart, no “Buy”, no “Request a quote”, no stock, no link to the maker’s own page.

In this walk nobody reached that button. Every product tap in every lane returned “Couldn’t load product” / “Let’s try that again”, and the failed screen exposes exactly one control — the retry link. There is no back chevron. The way out is to force-quit.

So the dead end has two floors: a screen that will not open, and, behind it, a screen that would have nothing to sell if it did.

The rail is already armed

A client-facing order table, an RPC that writes one, a Checkout branch that bills it, a webhook that settles it and emails the receipt — all of it shipped, none of it reachable from a phone. Nineteen of the twenty-one pieces in the local catalog already pass the RPC’s own buyability gate.

Three things are genuinely absent, and only one of them is a screen: the designer’s share of the sale, any state after “paid”, and the iOS act itself.

Stripe is configured on Strata — both secret names are present — but a name cannot tell a test key from a live one, and no device pass was available to this review. Treat the rail as configured and unverified-live.

The Apple rule, and why it is not in the way

Physical goods are not sold through in-app purchase. They are paid for outside it — guidelines 3.1.3(e) and 3.1.5(a) — and the compliant container is the one already on the phone: Stripe’s hosted Checkout, opened in SFSafariViewController, the rail the invoice screen has ridden since it shipped.

A sofa, then, costs nothing in review risk. The only line worth naming is the one neither direction crosses: a digital service sold inside the app — a consultation, say — would be a separate 3.1.1 conversation. The design fee the app hands to that same Checkout today is a service billed externally, and stays where it is.

What Apple decides, and what it does not

  • Decides: money for a physical piece leaves the app. Payment opens in Safari, and comes back with a receipt.
  • Decides: Apple Pay is a wallet inside that page, not a second integration. It is already accepted there.
  • Does not decide: who is credited for the sale, what the buyer is told before it, or what he is told after. Those are ours.

A native payment sheet would buy one fewer context switch and cost a dependency, a merchant entitlement, a new backend mode and a rewritten settle. It would not buy Apple Pay, which is already there.

After the money: what Tom has to know

The designer’s checklist starts where the buyer’s ends. Tom came in with three questions about a $3,200 sideboard bought inside his own client’s phone — who gets paid, who is responsible when it arrives damaged, and does it show up on his FF&E schedule — and found all three answered in the schema before he reached a screen.

Today the absence protects him: there is nothing to buy. What does not protect him is a finished rail with no column for his name on it.

Three paths, and the thing that picks between them

A piece can be bought, sourced, or asked about. Which one a person is offered is not a badge on their account — it is whether a designer is already working for them. Both directions arrive at the same three paths and split on one line of it, which is a ruling, not a design.

PathWho sees itWhen it appliesWhat exists todayWhat it would take
A · Buy it guest, at a sign-in wall on the tap · discovering · any client with no designer working for them The piece is sellable by Patina, and nobody is already sourcing for this person. Plus attribution columns, which are free now.
B · Ask your designer to source it engaged · activeProject A designer is on the job. The piece belongs on her schedule and the margin is hers. Nothing on the piece. Her rail is proposals, invoices and the same hosted Checkout, raised in the portal. A structured message into the project thread naming the piece, its price and the room.
C · Ask about this piece any tier The price is missing, the piece is made to order, or it fails the sellable test. Nothing. There is no price inquiry, no maker link, and no “ask” anywhere on a piece. The same sheet, writing to the existing lead rail with the piece attached — and, where a designer exists, into her thread rather than a second lead.

Both directions state the rule the same way: a live designer relationship decides the path, not the tier. They differ on what happens next. One never draws Buy for a client who has a designer; the other draws it underneath “Ask Leah to source this”, with a line saying she is credited and his price is unchanged. That difference is put to a ruling, with the attribution columns landing either way.
