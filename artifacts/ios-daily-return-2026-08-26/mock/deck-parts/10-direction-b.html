Direction B — The Record

The thing worth returning to is the house, not the catalogue. The app opens on a dated record of what moved while you were away — who did it, what it costs, what is waiting on you — and the pieces are a door off it.

The name is the product’s own editorial copy, seeded and unreachable: “Patina is not a finish. It is a record.” The one genuinely variable thing Patina owns is that another person did something about your house — a proposal raised, a decision asked, an invoice due, a piece shipped. All of it is computed on the backend today and drawn on no screen a returning person lands on, while the home is built around idle browsing whose strongest surface does not load and cannot take money. B inverts the weight.

The purchase path, and the designer on the job

One rule decides all of it: the designer on the job owns the piece. Where a designer is engaged the piece leads with “Ask Leah to source this”; buying it yourself is the secondary act, credited to her at the piece’s own trade rate, with a line that says so and says it does not change your price.

The gate. Where there is no designer, Buy draws — but only on a piece with a positive price, a seller of record, dimensions, a lead time, a resolvable maker and an image verified against it. Everything else falls to the ask. That turns an omit‑when‑null rendering rule into a structural one: a $4,200 order sheet cannot ship missing the two facts a buyer leads with, over a photograph of somebody else’s chairs.

This is the answer to my own question: if this ships as-is, a client can buy the same piece I sourced directly through Patina, I get zero commission, it never lands on my FF&E schedule, and I find out only when something goes wrong with the delivery — the exact scenario I’ve already been burned by once, now built into the platform I recommend to clients.
