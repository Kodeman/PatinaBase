One

The short version

Leah's team asked for two things. The first is to adjust the client price piece by piece — taking the edge off a hero piece and spreading that margin across the room. The second is to see a verified-pricing date beside every cost. Today Patina can do neither.

A designer types one field called Unit Price, and it lands in the trade column. The client price exists only after a bulk Apply markup % is run over a batch of selected lines. Every new line, however it is born, starts at zero margin with the same number in both columns. The only place trade, markup, client and line margin appear together is a lens titled Financial lens · studio only, gated to the studio owner, set in type below the product's own floor. And no price anywhere carries a date, a source, or an expiry: the database has no column for it anywhere.

The trade says both asks are ordinary practice. Studios plan to a project-level blended margin and deliberately run lower markup on the big pieces and higher on the accessories (R2). Vendor quotes are good for about thirty days, and the 2025–26 run of tariff and materials surcharges made an undated price a liability rather than a nuisance (R3). Of the eight design-business tools checked, none of the eight documents a first-class blend and none documents a priced on stamp — an absence in reachable public documentation, not a demonstrated absence in the products. This is Patina's to do first, on the evidence we could reach.

Eleven proposals in three waves. Wave one fixes the floor — five defects that make every margin figure in the product wrong today, and which no new feature can be built on top of. Wave two prices with intent — the Blend, the priced-on stamp, an honest release sheet. Wave three keeps it honest — price history, post-sale edits, library intake. Eleven rulings follow, each naming who rules.

Nothing in this document resolves vision ruling V1, Patina's own margin pocket. Every dollar discussed here is the studio's own money, on the studio's own rail.

Two

What Leah's team asked for

The feedback, as it reached us — the gist, not a verbatim transcript:

When we build a job, a proposal, or a purchase approval, we like to adjust the client cost per piece. Sometimes that means taking the edge off a more expensive piece and spreading some of that margin across other items. We'd also like to see verified pricing dates next to our cost.

There are three needs inside that, and they want different things from the product.

Control

Shape the client price on a single line, in the client's own number, without selecting a batch and running a percentage over it. The principal in the simulated panel put the same thing in the negative. The client price is decided first, in her head or in a spreadsheet. The tool's job is to record that decision, not to derive it.

A held total

The room's number does not move when a line does. Taking $740 off the sofa is not a discount; it is a redistribution, and the studio expects to see it land somewhere.

Trust in the cost

When was this true, who said so, and until when. Not because a designer forgets, but because six weeks later a client asks why something went up, and a quote number reads better than a memory.

What was not asked

Nothing about the client seeing margin. Nothing about Patina's own take. Both stay exactly where they are: the client's documents keep their four columns, and the platform's commission rail is not touched by anything in this document.

Four

What the trade does

Everything in this section comes from research lanes R1, R2 and R3, and every figure carries the source that produced it. All external pages were accessed 5 September 2026. Where a range was asserted by practitioner-adjacent business writing rather than a survey, it is named as such rather than dressed up.

Models

No single pricing model dominates. Hybrids are widely asserted across practitioner-adjacent writing rather than survey-verified: a flat or hourly design fee for the creative and project-management work, layered with cost-plus markup on the furnishings. Cost-plus and retail-minus are the two dominant procurement mechanics — the first charges net cost plus a stated percentage, the second charges retail and keeps the spread. A worked example of the first, from Capella Kincheloe: $8999 (retail) minus designer discount of 40% = $5399.40 (designer's net) x 35% markup = $7289.19. The second, from the same page: The designer pays the designer net price, and the client will always pay retail and the designer's fee is the difference.

Markup on cost and margin on price are different numbers and are routinely conflated by designers themselves. Interior Design Community works it plainly: A piece that costs you $1,000 and sells for $1,600 carries a 60 percent markup. But the profit on that sale, $600, is only 37.5 percent of the $1,600 you actually collected. 35% on cost is 25.9% on price — 0.35 ÷ 1.35, derived here rather than cited. This is the distinction a tool must keep straight, and it is the reason every figure in this document carries the words on cost or on price.

Category ranges, cited by practitioners rather than measured: general FF&E around 30–40% markup on cost; custom upholstery higher; case goods 40–60% and described as the most price-checkable category. DesignFiles puts the industry average at 35% with most designers between 30 and 35. No survey was found that breaks down what share of studios use which model, and none of the category ranges traces to a named methodology. They are what the trade says out loud, and this document treats them as directional.

Disclosure

Disclosure is a live legal question, not a courtesy. The ASID Code of Ethics requires members to fully disclose to a client all compensation that the member shall receive in connection with the project. Design Ink Co is explicit that generic contract language fails the test — 'Industry-standard markup' is not considered sufficient disclosure — and reports Virginia litigation in which undisclosed markups were found to be a breach of agreement and, separately, a consumer-protection violation. The safer contract pattern names the number: Client will be charged the net cost plus a procurement fee of X% on approved furnishings. Trade discounts, if any, are retained by the designer as part of studio compensation.

The implication for Patina is narrow and worth stating. The client documents already never show trade, and nothing here changes that. What the studio must be able to do is state its own practice precisely — which requires the studio's own number to be legible to the studio, on one definition, at the moment of the decision. A tool that leaves four disagreeing margin figures in the product is not helping a designer honor a cost-plus clause.

Blending

Blending is a named practice, not an improvisation. The clearest statement found, from Interior Design Community: you need about a 40% margin on the overall project to be profitable, but…that 40% is on the project as a whole, so if you can't do it in some places you can make it up in others. The same piece reports designers running 30–40% on smaller projects and 20–30% on larger ones, reasoning about overhead recovery per project rather than per item, and describes the tracking mechanism as a basic spreadsheet that totals cost against sale price as you build out a proposal — running beside whatever software the studio pays for.

The shape of the blend — cap the markup on the hero piece a client can price-check, lift it on the lighting and accessories nobody will — is consistent across every source touched but is stated cleanly by none of them; R2 marks it inferred synthesis. Two hard edges are external rather than editorial. MAP is a vendor-enforced floor on advertised price: going below it can cost a studio its trade account. The ceiling is the maker's own published retail — a client price above it is not a thing a studio will do — and the practical top is around 2.2–2.5× wholesale (Procurist, figures paraphrased). And rounding is barely engineered anywhere — none of the eight products documents an automatic rule, and while professional-services pricing plainly favours $0 and $5 endings over $.99, no source names a specific increment as a convention. R4's recommendation of $5 is a studio setting with a default, not an appeal to practice.

Freight, receiving and install are handled inconsistently, with no dominant convention — sometimes a separately disclosed line, sometimes a figure folded into the goods markup — with survey answers ranging from a 10% processing fee to a 25% flat rate to no markup at all. No product forces either convention; each simply provides fields.

Price validity

Thirty days is the modal quote window. Generic B2B practice, per LawInsider's aggregated clause samples: Quotations are valid for 30 days unless otherwise stated. A custom upholstery workroom's published terms say the same and give the reason: All quotes provided are valid for 30 days from the date of issue. After this period, prices may be subject to change based on fluctuations in material costs or availability (Creature Comforts Upholstery). A manufacturer's terms add the second date the vendors themselves draft: acceptance within 30 days, but prices guaranteed only for materials that can be scheduled and shipped within 150 days (Murdock Manufacturing). Subject to change without notice is boilerplate on nearly every terms page found.

What made an undated price a liability rather than a nuisance was the 2025–26 repricing. A 25% Section 232 tariff on imported upholstered wooden furniture, cabinets and vanities took effect 14 October 2025, and a scheduled increase was delayed to 1 January 2027 (GHY). Surcharges arrived mid-cycle and separately from list price — tariff surcharges of 3.5% to 8% across the named cases, reported as aggregated trade coverage and not re-verifiable at a single URL, plus a 5% fuel surcharge and reports of MDF and solid wood up 15–17%, both of those attributed to Home Accents Today. Living, kitchen and dining furniture inflation moved from about 1% year over year in early 2025 to a 9.5% peak that August before cooling, on the furniture-and-bedding series (Statista).

Designers changed behavior in response, on the record. Business of Home quotes Kevin Twitty — it's somewhere around 30 percent more expensive this year to do a remodel than it was last year — with clients still spending the same, so what that does is it shrinks our projects or updates our scope. Lindsay Olson describes disclosing exposure at presentation rather than after, and estimates spending 20 to 25 percent more time re-sourcing to avoid tariffs. Winn Galloway names the real pressure: It's not necessarily that the higher costs have been the problem; it really is cash flow. The sharpest practitioner-facing checklist found is a quote-review one: look at the expiration date, tariff language, escalation provisions, and what happens if the purchase order is issued after the quote expires (Furngully).

No design-business tool checked documents a first-class price verified on or quote expires field. The precedents are all adjacent. Aisle Planner, in event planning, has a literal Expiration Date field on quotes and proposals, and an expired document shows the client a message prompting them to ask for an updated version — the clearest named precedent for exactly this interface, from the wrong vertical. NetSuite's Effective Date Pricing is the mature data-model precedent: a price carries a start date and an optional end date per pricing level. And camelcamelcamel discloses its own lag rather than hiding it, which is the honesty posture worth copying. One caution from the freshness-badge literature is worth carrying into the build: A badge showing the wrong number is worse than no badge at all (Windmill). A verified stamp must be reliably computed or it damages the thing it is meant to build.

Synthesizing across all of it, a trustworthy stamp carries five things: date (when the price was confirmed, distinct from when the row was last written), who, source (quote number, price-list revision, website, phone, catalog), validity, and what changed since. P4 and P5 below are that list, split at the line where a database change stops being small.

Five

Principles

Eight, in the order they get argued about. Each is a rule the proposals below are checked against, not a sentiment.

Surface · The Document — the Drafting Room's FF&E facet, the project FF&E schedule, and the release sheet.

Studio moment · the studio adding its first hands while its workload doubles.

Stream · the upside stream — margin on furniture sold through designer-led projects — which this document leaves entirely to the studio. Patina is paid here on the subscription floor.

Promise · the studio won't notice Patina: fewer spreadsheet detours, and nothing in here optimized for engagement.

One · the client price is the number

Markup is a note about it. The product already says this after the sale — markup_percent is advisory… Client price is the source of truth 00185:62-65. Pre-sale disagrees today. Make pre-sale agree.

Two · the total holds

A move on one line is paid for by the others, visibly, in the same act. If a designer wants the room to come down, that is a different act with a different name, and she should have to perform it.

Three · say the date or say nothing

A price with no verified date reads unverified. It never implies freshness it does not have. This is the C5 convention already written into photo_verified_at 00533:54-55, applied to money.

Four · trade never travels

Every client surface keeps the columns it has. Nothing in this document widens what a homeowner sees about money, and the four-columns-and-no-fifth rule holds on both the proposal and the authorization.

Five · quiet when fresh

Aging is a change of ink, not a banner. No red anywhere; terracotta is the strongest word the page has. The simulated principal's caution is the reason: a warning-styled treatment changes how designers enter prices, which defeats the point.

Six · every move leaves a trace

A blend, a verify, a price change — each is a line in the record, in plain words a bookkeeper can read three months later without phoning the designer.

Seven · the studio decides who sees margin

Not the tool's default. A silent permission wall reads as a locked door to a junior designer; a stated rule reads as ordinary. The wording is ruled on in R1.

Eight · none of this is Patina's money

Rail A only. Vision ruling V1 — whether Patina ever takes a margin pocket of its own — stays open and is not touched, argued, or quietly presumed anywhere in this document.

Ten

Open rulings

Eleven. Each carries the recommendation this team would make if forced, and who rules. Where a ruling is Leah's, it is because the answer is a practice question and the tool should follow the practice. Where it is Kody's, it is because the answer sets a contract the product has to keep.

R1 · who sees margin

Owners only, as today, or everyone in the studio?

The lens is gated to the studio owner and titled studio only, while the bulk markup bar that sets the number sits outside the gate entirely. The first hire builds the schedule and cannot see what her work is worth; she also cannot tell whether that is a rule or an accident. A simulated junior-designer walk of a different screen in an earlier program made the same observation about a different string, at low confidence [simulated]. An unexplained wall reads as suspicion; a stated rule reads as ordinary.

RecommendationA studio setting, defaulting to everyone in the studio, with owners able to restrict. Whichever way it lands, the page says which in words rather than by hiding a tab. And setting and reading move behind the same gate, so a member can never apply a markup she is not allowed to see.

Leah rules the practice · Kody rules the default and the gate · if the two disagree, the practice wins and the default is changed to follow it

R2 · the entry frame

Markup on cost, discount off retail, or both?

Design Manager is the only product in the researched set offering markup-on-cost, discount-off-list and a flat purchasing fee as parallel methods. The case for discount-off-retail is real: a price entered as retail minus a discount can never exceed the maker's published retail, which is the ceiling the fixture's locked rug is already up against. The case against is that it is a second mental frame in a product whose research finding number three is that designers already conflate the two they have.

RecommendationThe field is client price. Markup on cost is shown beside it; margin on price appears in totals with the words on price attached. No discount-off-retail entry in v1. Revisit when a studio asks for it by name.

Kody rules

R3 · what the Blend holds

Does the sheet hold the client total, or the blended percentage?

They are different tools. Holding the total is the motion described in the feedback: take the edge off the sofa, the room's number does not move. Holding the percentage is a planning tool: set the room to 35% and let the sheet propose a split. The mockup shows both as a mode switch, which is a hedge, and hedges cost more than they look.

RecommendationThe total, with the percentage as a readout. Build hold the % as a second mode only if the team asks for it after using the first.

Leah rules

R4 · rounding

None, $1, $5, $10, or $25?

Every direct search for a named industry convention came back empty; R2 reports this as not confirmed. What is documented is that professional-services pricing favours $0 and $5 endings rather than $.99, and that designers round by hand to something that reads clean. The simulated principal's own example [simulated] was $2,450 rather than $2,438.60.

RecommendationA studio setting, default $5, applied to client unit prices only. The remainder from a blend lands on the smallest unlocked line so the room reconciles exactly, and the footer says it did.

Leah rules

R5 · a client price below trade

Store a NULL, store a negative markup, or refuse the entry?

It happens: a piece thrown in at cost, a correction, a goodwill gesture on a job that went long. Today markup_percent carries no CHECK pre-sale, so a negative is storable, and activation then clamps it to zero 00279:199-200,237-238 — the number survives the proposal and dies at the project boundary.

RecommendationAllow it. Show the negative with its sign, warn at the floor, and stop the clamping as part of F5. A studio that gave something away should be able to see that it did.

Kody rules

R6 · price-age thresholds

Is 60 and 90 the right pair, and does it vary by studio?

Thirty days is the modal vendor quote window, and the product's one shipping staleness rule already uses ninety spec-books/model.ts:442-444. Sixty and ninety give a designer a nudge before the quote is actually dead. But a studio that works mostly in custom upholstery, where a workroom holds a price for thirty days flat, will want tighter.

RecommendationA studio setting, defaults 60 aging, 90 stale. A stated validity date always wins over the thresholds — if a quote says it expires October 11, the line goes stale on October 11 regardless of its age.

Leah rules

R7 · the client and dates

Does the client see “priced as of”, or only “good through”?

The simulated homeowner persona [simulated] wanted the feeling rather than the mechanics: something small that says somebody checked, recently, before asking her to sign. A per-line date delivers that and also invites a question about the line without one. A single good-through line delivers most of it and invites nothing.

RecommendationGood through only — on the proposal now, and on the authorization once that document carries a validity date of its own — never a per-line date on a client surface. Aisle Planner's expired-document message is the precedent for what happens after the date, and is worth reading before writing ours.

Leah rules · counsel on the wording

R8 · post-sale edits

Where exactly does a price stop being editable?

The database has already drawn most of this line and drawn it only around the client's money. The soft lock fires on a sent authorization; the executed freeze requires an exact match to the signed snapshot; neither mentions trade price or markup. So today a studio can revise its own cost on a signed line and cannot revise the client's, which is defensible, and can also do it with no record, which is not.

RecommendationEditable until a line sits on a sent authorization; then void-to-edit, in the words the database already uses; signed means a change order. Trade price and markup stay editable after signature, but through a guarded write path and into the record, so a bookkeeper can see it happened.

Kody rules · absorbs the queued post-sale editor ruling

R9 · the floor

Does a margin floor warn, or block?

The fixture trips its own floor on purpose. Leah takes the sofa to 19% on price knowing what she is doing, and a tool that stopped her there would be wrong about who is running the studio.

The treatment already has a precedent in the product, on the other rail. The check is belowFloor = marginPct < config.marginFloorPct, on the config key margin_floor_warning, set at 25%. Its own header comment says the strip and the commission block render terracotta below the margin floor packages/fulfillment/src/money.ts:53,76-77. Copy the treatment, and say plainly what it is. That floor is a different number on a different rail that happens to share a value. It warns on Patina's own projected commission margin, not on the studio's margin on furnishings.

RecommendationWarn, never block. The studio prices; the tool remarks. One quiet line on the row, in terracotta, with the number in it.
