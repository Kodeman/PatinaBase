# Pricing mechanics panel — four voices on trade cost, client price, and trust

Program: pricing mechanics (Patina). Lane: R5. This document interviews four synthetic personas
built from the feature test in `docs/vision/VISION.md`, the money block of
`leah-session-05-founders-sitdown.html`, and the verified facts about how Patina prices today. None
of these people exist. Nothing here is evidence about Leah's studio, her first hire, her
bookkeeper, or any homeowner. It exists to turn four vague worries ("we like to adjust the price,"
"we'd like to see verified dates") into specific questions a real conversation can answer in one
sitting.

---

## Persona 1 — The principal

*Runs a 3-person residential studio in the Midwest. 12 years in. Holds every trade account herself.
Still prices in a spreadsheet next to whatever software she's using, because she doesn't trust any
tool with the last mile of a number.*

**(a) How I build price today, step by step.**

I get the trade cost from the rep — quote, invoice, sometimes just a phone call I write down myself.
That number goes in my spreadsheet first, always, because the spreadsheet is where the real math
happens. I've got a markup that's not one number — it moves. A $400 lamp gets a bigger multiple than
an $8,000 sofa, because nobody wants to see a four-figure markup sitting next to "lamp" on a page,
even if the percentage is identical. Then I round the client price to something that reads clean —
$2,450, not $2,438.60 — because a client who sees a weird number thinks you didn't do the math, even
when you did more math than they'll ever know. Only after all that do I go back and open the actual
tool and type in whatever the spreadsheet told me.

**(b) Where it breaks — the specific moment.**

The moment is: I've already decided the client price in my head, in the spreadsheet, before I ever
touch the software. The tool wants me to type a trade cost and a markup and let it hand me a client
price. But that's backwards from how I actually think. I think in the client number first — what
does this room need to land at, what does this piece need to cost so the whole schedule feels fair
— and the trade cost and margin are what's left over. When the only way to move a client price is
that bulk "Apply markup %" bar over a batch of lines, I can't do the thing I actually do, which is
take four hundred dollars off the sofa because I already spent the client's patience on the rug, and
put forty of it back on three side tables nobody's going to notice. Right now that means I go do it
in the spreadsheet and then hand-key three separate markup percentages into three separate lines to
back into the number I already decided. That's not adjusting price. That's translating a decision
I've already made into a language the tool understands.

**(c) What I'd need to see beside a number to trust it.**

I need to see my own math confirmed, not overridden. If I put in a client price, I want the tool to
show me the margin that results — not fight me for control of which field is "the real one." And I
want a date on the trade cost. Not because I don't remember — because six weeks from now when a
client asks "why did this go up," I need to point at something better than my memory. A quote number
next to the date would save me an email to the rep every single time.

**(d) What must the client never see; what would make me distrust the designer.**

The client never sees my markup, obviously — not the percentage, not the dollar spread, not even a
hint that a spread exists. That's not embarrassment, it's professional boundary; a general
contractor doesn't itemize his overhead either. What would make me distrust the *tool* — because
that's really the question here — is if I found out later it had done something to a price I didn't
tell it to do. Auto-adjusted, auto-expired, auto-anything with a client-facing number. If I set it,
it stays set until I touch it again.

**(e) Reactions to the four sketches.**

[S1] Trade and Client columns side by side, edit either, markup follows — yes. That's the field I've
been keying into a spreadsheet for twelve years. Let me edit the client column directly and stop
doing translation work.

[S2] The Blend sheet, target for the room, drag the sofa down, spread across the rest, total holds —
this is the actual motion. This is what I do by hand every single proposal. If it does the arithmetic
and I still get to see and touch every line before it locks, I'd use it immediately.

[S3] The verified-date line, amber past 60, terracotta past 90 — good, with one condition: don't
make it loud. A quiet mono line is fine. If it turns into a banner or a badge that makes the page look
like something's wrong when nothing's wrong yet, I'll start hiding real prices behind round numbers
just to avoid the color.

[S4] Studio default markup with category defaults — careful here. A default is a floor for a junior
designer who doesn't know better yet, and I like that for my first hire. But if it ever quietly
overrides what I typed on a line I've already priced by hand, that's the thing that breaks trust
fastest. Defaults for new lines only, never for lines a human has already touched.

## Persona 2 — The first hire

*Junior designer, 2 years in. Builds the FF&E schedule, drafts proposals. Not sure what she's
allowed to see or change about margin — nobody's told her the rule out loud.*

**(a) How I read and build price today, step by step.**

I build the schedule — item, room, trade cost, whatever markup the line already has, which is
usually zero because that's what a new line starts at. I don't touch markup unless someone tells me
a number. Usually the number comes from a Slack message or my boss walking by and saying "make that
one 45, make the rug 30." I type those in one at a time. Then I look at the client-facing view to
make sure it looks clean before I send anything anywhere.

**(b) Where it breaks — the specific moment.**

The moment I get nervous is when I've drafted a whole room's worth of lines at zero markup because
nobody's given me numbers yet, and the client-facing proposal is due today. I don't know if I'm
supposed to guess a markup, wait, or send it at zero — which I'm pretty sure means the client would
see the same number as the trade cost, and I don't know if that's a real bug or just what happens
when nobody's finished the job. The second moment is the "Financial lens" table. I can see it. I
don't know if I'm supposed to be able to see it. Nobody's told me "you're cleared for this" or "don't
touch this" — I just found the tab and I've been quietly leaving it alone because it feels like
looking at someone's paycheck.

**(c) What I'd need to see beside a number to trust it.**

I'd want the tool to just tell me, in plain words, whether a line is done or still needs a markup —
not make me infer it from a zero. And on the margin table, I want it to say who's supposed to be
here, in words, not just put the table where I happen to be able to click it. If it's fine for me to
see it, say so. If it's not, don't show it to me at all — don't make me guess whether I'm trespassing.

**(d) What must the client never see; what would make me distrust the designer.**

The client can never see a zero-markup line sitting next to a fully-priced one — that reads like we
forgot about half the room, or worse, like some clients are paying more than others for the exact
same kind of decision. That's the part that would embarrass me, not the margin itself. What would
make me distrust the *system* is if it let me send a proposal with an incomplete line in it without
so much as a flag. I've done that already, once, before someone caught it. Nobody yelled, but I
felt sick.

**(e) Reactions to the four sketches.**

[S1] Trade and Client columns side by side — I like this only if I still can't move the trade column
without permission. I'm allowed near client price sometimes; I've never touched what a piece costs
us on paper. If both columns are just open fields with no difference in who can edit what, that
scares me a little.

[S2] The Blend sheet — I'd want someone to have set the room target already before I'm allowed to
drag anything. Otherwise I'm the one deciding how much margin comes off the sofa, and that's not my
call to make at two years in.

[S3] The verified-date line — I love this one without reservation. Half my job is chasing down
whether a quote is still good. If the page just tells me, that's an hour a week back.

[S4] Studio default markup with category defaults — this is the one I actually asked for without
knowing it had a name. If new lines started at a real number instead of zero, I'd stop having that
nervous feeling about sending something half-priced. It also tells me, without anyone having to say
it out loud, what "normal" looks like around here.

## Persona 3 — The bookkeeper / ops

*Part-time. Reconciles POs to proposals to invoices. Cares about what was promised versus what was
paid.*

**(a) How I read price today, step by step.**

I open the proposal the client signed, I open the PO that went to the maker, and I open whatever
invoice came back from Stripe or the maker directly, and I check that three numbers agree: what the
client approved, what we told the maker to build at, and what actually got charged. When they don't
match I have to go find out why, usually by asking the designer, because nothing on the page tells
me why a number moved.

**(b) Where it breaks — the specific moment.**

The moment is always the same: a number is different from what I remember approving, and there's no
paper trail on the page that says why. Did the trade cost go up because the maker requoted? Did the
designer take margin off the sofa on purpose? Did someone edit a locked line and I missed the
notification? Right now I can't tell those three situations apart just by looking — I have to go ask
a person, and the person doesn't always remember either, three months later. "Prices lock when you
release" is good as far as it goes, but locked doesn't tell me what the price *was* before, or why it
became what it is.

**(c) What I'd need to see beside a number to trust it.**

A history, even a short one. Not a full audit log with timestamps down to the second — just: this
line was $2,450, someone changed it to $2,300 on this date, here's roughly why (trade cost changed /
designer adjusted / bulk markup applied). And the verified-pricing date matters to me more than to
anyone else in this room, because when a maker's invoice comes in three weeks after the quote
expired, I need to know that's expected, not a red flag.

**(d) What must the client never see; what would make me distrust the designer.**

The client should never see two different "final" numbers for the same line at different times
without an explanation attached to at least one of them. That's the thing that turns into a hard
conversation with a homeowner. What would make me distrust the *designer* — not the tool — is if I
found trade cost moving without a source. A price with no quote number and no date behind it is just
a guess, and I can't reconcile a guess.

**(e) Reactions to the four sketches.**

[S1] Trade and Client columns side by side — good for me only if editing one leaves a trace of what
the other used to be. Otherwise it's the same blind spot I have now, just with a nicer layout.

[S2] The Blend sheet — this is the one I actually worry about. If the difference "spreads across the
unlocked lines" automatically, I need every one of those small movements to still show up somewhere
when I go looking, even if it's just "adjusted via Blend, room: primary suite, date." An invisible
redistribution across six lines is exactly the kind of thing that turns into a two-hour phone call
in month four.

[S3] The verified-date line — yes, immediately, and I'd want the same thing on the PO and the
invoice, not just the proposal, so I can eyeball whether the quote that got paid is the quote that
got approved.

[S4] Studio default markup — doesn't affect me directly, but I'd want to know it exists so that a
line I'm reconciling that looks "off" from a category default isn't a red flag by itself — it might
just be a designer doing exactly what she's allowed to do.

## Persona 4 — The homeowner

*Reading the proposal and the purchase approval. Cares about fairness, surprises, and whether the
number will hold.*

**(a) How I read price today, step by step.**

Honestly, I read it like a restaurant bill after a big dinner — I look at the total first, then I
scan down the list to see if anything jumps out as weird, then I look at the total again. I trust my
designer. I'm not trying to audit her. I just want to feel like the number in front of me is the
number I'm going to see when the truck shows up.

**(b) Where it breaks — the specific moment.**

If a price changes between when I approve it and when it actually gets delivered, and nobody told me
why, that's the moment. I don't need to know what she pays for things — that's not my business, and
frankly I'd feel a little weird knowing it, like I peeked at a menu's food cost. But if the sofa I
signed off on in March shows up on an invoice in July at a different number, with no explanation
anywhere, I start wondering what else I wasn't told.

**(c) What I'd need to see beside a number to trust it.**

Something small that tells me this number is current — not stale, not a guess from six months ago.
I wouldn't need to understand "verified Aug 12, quote #4471, good through Oct 1" in detail, but
just seeing a recent-looking date next to the price would tell me somebody checked, recently, before
asking me to sign. That's the whole ask. I don't need the mechanics. I need the confidence.

**(d) What must the client never see; what would make me distrust the designer.**

I should never see her markup or her cost — full stop, and honestly I don't want to. What would make
me distrust her is the opposite of a hidden number: it's an unexplained *changed* number. Silence
around a price move reads as something being hidden from me, even if nothing shady happened at all.
A short, plain sentence — "your sofa's price firmed up when the fabric came off backorder" — would
fix it completely. The absence of that sentence is the whole problem.

**(e) Reactions to the four sketches.**

[S1] Trade and Client columns — I never see this, so no reaction; it's not built for me and it
shouldn't be.

[S2] The Blend sheet — same, this is a studio tool, not mine. What I'd care about is only the
outcome: does my total still add up the way she told me it would.

[S3] The verified-date line — again, not something I'd expect to see literally on my copy of the
purchase approval, but the *feeling* it produces — recency, someone checked — that's exactly the
feeling I want from whatever I'm signing. If a client-safe version of that ever showed up on my
side, plain language, no jargon, I'd like it.

[S4] Studio default markup — invisible to me and should stay that way.

## Ranked needs across personas (top 10)

Each need is tagged with the persona(s) who raised it and the dimension it belongs to: **control**
(who can move a number and how), **trust** (proof a number is real and current), **visibility**
(who is and isn't meant to see what), or **speed** (how many steps to do the thing they already do).

1. **A room-level "spread the difference" motion that preserves the total.** [Principal, raised
   directly as the thing she already does by hand; Bookkeeper, conditionally, if it stays
   traceable] — **control**. This is the single largest gap between how price is actually decided
   (holistically, client-price-first) and how the tool makes you enter it (line-by-line,
   trade-cost-first).

2. **A verified-pricing date visible next to every cost, with a source reference.** [Principal,
   Bookkeeper, First hire, Homeowner (in spirit, client-safe form)] — **trust**. Raised by every
   persona in some form; the closest thing to a universal need in this panel.

3. **A per-line client-price field that doesn't require a bulk-select workflow.** [Principal] —
   **speed / control**. The "Apply markup %" bar forces batch thinking onto a decision that is often
   made one line at a time, out of order, against a target the designer already has in her head.

4. **A visible, human-readable reason attached to any price change.** [Bookkeeper, primarily;
   Homeowner, in a client-safe form] — **trust**. Not a full audit log — a short "why," so
   reconciliation and client confidence don't depend on someone's memory three months later.

5. **A clear, stated rule about who can see the margin table — stated in words, not inferred from
   who can click into it.** [First hire] — **visibility**. Directly echoes F150 from the prior
   panel: a permission wall reads as suspicious when it's silent, and reads as fine when it's
   explicit. The fix here is not removing the label — it's the tool telling a junior designer
   plainly whether she's cleared, rather than her guessing from access alone.

6. **A studio default markup (with category overrides) so new lines never sit at zero.** [Principal,
   qualified; First hire, unqualified enthusiasm] — **control / trust**. Popular with the persona
   who currently has no rule to follow, more cautiously received by the persona who doesn't want a
   default to silently override her own hand-priced line.

7. **A flag on any client-facing line that is still at zero markup or otherwise "unfinished."**
   [First hire] — **visibility**. Distinct from need 6 — this is about catching an incomplete state
   before it ships, not about what the default number should be.

8. **Redistribution moves in a Blend-style tool must leave a visible trace per line, not just a new
   total.** [Bookkeeper] — **trust**. The one clear caution on S2: an invisible spread across six
   lines is a reconciliation problem waiting to happen, even though the room-level motion itself
   (need 1) is wanted.

9. **A client-safe, plain-language note when a delivered price differs from an approved price.**
   [Homeowner] — **trust**. The homeowner's one concrete need; distinct from needs 2 and 4 in that it
   must never carry a number the client isn't meant to see (trade cost, margin), only an explanation.

10. **A quiet, non-alarming visual treatment for aging price data (amber/terracotta), not a banner
    or badge.** [Principal] — **visibility / trust**. A caution on the mechanism proposed in S3: the
    information is wanted, but if it's styled as a warning it will change designer behavior (e.g.
    rounding or avoiding real numbers to dodge the color) in ways that defeat the point.

## Questions to put to the real team (Leah's studio) — at most 12, each answerable in one sentence

1. When you take margin off one line and spread it across others, do you decide the *room's total*
   first, or the *individual line's client price* first?

2. Today, when you want to move a client price without touching a bulk markup bar, what do you
   actually do — spreadsheet, sticky note, memory?

3. If a new FF&E line started at a studio-set default markup instead of zero, would that save you
   time or would it fight the way you price?

4. Does your first hire currently know whether she's allowed to see the margin table — and if she
   isn't, what happens if she stumbles onto it?

5. When a maker's invoice comes in at a different number than the signed proposal, how do you find
   out why today, and how long does that usually take?

6. Would you want a verified-pricing date on every line, or only on lines above some dollar
   threshold?

7. If a price changes after a client signs, do you currently tell them why, and if so, how — email,
   call, nothing?

8. Would an amber-then-terracotta color change on an aging quote change how you price things — would
   you round numbers or delay entering a quote to avoid the color?

9. Who in your studio, besides you, is trusted to touch a client price on a live proposal?

10. When you spread margin across a room's unlocked lines, do you need to see that history later, or
    is the final number enough once it's locked?

11. Does your bookkeeper currently have a way to tell "trade cost changed" apart from "designer
    adjusted the margin on purpose" — or is that always a phone call?

12. Would a studio-level default markup need to vary by category (rugs vs. case goods vs. lighting)
    from day one, or would one flat default get you most of the way there?

## Words to avoid

Drawn from where a persona's reaction curdled — either it read as a permission wall, or it read as
the tool hiding something it shouldn't.

- **"STUDIO EYES ONLY"** — the exact phrasing from F150 in the prior panel; a junior designer reads
  an unexplained warning label as a locked door, not as neutral information.
- **"Locked"** used bare, with no stated reason — reads as punitive rather than protective. ("Prices
  lock when you release" survives because it explains *when*; a bare "locked" badge on a line would
  not.)
- **"Adjusted"** with no further word — the bookkeeper's whole complaint is that a number changed
  with nothing beside it; a UI label that just says "adjusted" recreates the exact silence she's
  already fighting.
- **"Flagged" / "Warning" / "Alert"** applied to an aging quote — the principal's caution on S3 is
  precisely that a warning-styled treatment changes designer behavior (rounding, avoidance) in ways
  a quiet mono line would not.
- **"Override"** — any language implying the system can silently override a human-entered number
  (via a default, a bulk action, or an automated re-price) is the single fastest way to lose the
  principal's trust, by her own account in (d).
- **"AI-priced" / "auto-priced" / any "-powered" framing on a price** — orthogonal to the panel's
  reactions but forbidden by house style regardless; also would read, in this specific context, as
  exactly the kind of silent-override behavior every persona distrusts.
- **"Margin" shown to a client under any label** — not a wording problem so much as a visibility
  one, but worth stating plainly: no synonym, softened or otherwise, makes a client-facing margin
  figure acceptable per (d) across every professional persona in this panel.
