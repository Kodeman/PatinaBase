# How Residential Interior Designers Build Client-Facing Pricing and Margin Into a Job

## Executive Summary
1. No single pricing model dominates; practitioners converge on hybrids: a flat or hourly design fee for creative/PM work, layered with cost-plus markup on furnishings, finishes, and fixtures (FF&E).
2. Cost-plus (trade/net cost + a stated markup, commonly 15–50%) and retail-minus (client pays retail, or retail less a stated discount, with the spread as designer profit) are the two dominant procurement-pricing mechanics; percentage-of-project-spend and pure commission-only are rarer and mostly used by architects/GCs.
3. "Markup on cost" and "margin on price" are mathematically different and frequently conflated by designers themselves — a 35% markup yields roughly a 26% margin, and a 60% markup yields only ~37.5% margin — a distinction several practitioner-educators (LuAnn Nigara, Interior Design Community) treat as a recurring point of confusion.
4. Category markups vary widely by source: general FF&E commonly cited at 30–40% markup on cost; custom upholstery is cited as supporting 60–100%; case goods 40–60%; freight/receiving fees are handled inconsistently (flat rate, percentage, or pass-through at cost) with no industry standard.
5. Disclosure is a live legal and ethical issue, not just a courtesy: ASID's Code of Ethics requires members to "fully disclose to a client all compensation" received in connection with a project, and litigation (cited in Virginia) has found undisclosed markups and rebates to be contract/consumer-protection violations.
6. "Industry-standard markup" as boilerplate contract language is explicitly called out by at least one design-business consultant as insufficient disclosure; the safer pattern names a specific procurement-fee percentage and states that trade discounts are retained by the designer.
7. What the client sees varies by studio: some show itemized cost/markup/total columns per line, some show room subtotals only, and some collapse everything into a single "investment" figure — practitioner guidance leans toward itemization to reduce rejection risk and enable selective approval.
8. Document types cluster around three roles: the Proposal (client-facing, estimated, collects deposit), the Purchase Order (vendor-facing, finalizes actual cost once ordered), and the Invoice (client-facing, reflects final/actual billing) — software platforms (Studio Designer, Houzz Pro/Ivy) both encode and reinforce this three-document model.
9. Practitioner writing shows designers reasoning in at least three different mental frames simultaneously — markup-on-cost, margin-on-price, and "what a client will pay relative to retail/MSRP" — with business coaches (Capella Kincheloe, Sandra Funk, Dakota Design Co) pushing designers toward blended, whole-project margin targets rather than a single uniform per-item number.
10. No number-bearing survey was found breaking down the share of firms using each pricing model, nor Midwest-specific markup data; where sources gave ranges without a defensible survey behind them, this report states the range and names the (non-survey) source rather than inventing statistical weight.

## 1. Pricing models in use

### Verified

**Cost-plus / markup-on-net-cost.** The client is charged the designer's net (trade) cost plus a stated percentage. Capella Kincheloe's worked example: "$8999 (retail) minus designer discount of 40% = $5399.40 (designer's net) x 35% markup = $7289.19 (cost-plus, client price)" ([Capella Kincheloe](https://www.capellakincheloe.com/blog/pricing-interior-design-services), accessed 2026-09-05). Design Ink Co's framing of the same mechanic for contracts: "Client will be charged the net cost plus a procurement fee of X% on approved furnishings. Trade discounts, if any, are retained by the designer as part of studio compensation" ([Design Ink Co](https://www.designinkco.com/blog/interior-design-markup-disclosure), accessed 2026-09-05).

**Retail-minus / retail model.** The client pays retail (or retail less an agreed discount), and the designer's margin is the spread between what the client pays and the designer's net cost. Kincheloe: "The designer pays the designer net price, and the client will always pay retail and the designer's fee is the difference" ([Capella Kincheloe](https://www.capellakincheloe.com/blog/pricing-interior-design-services), accessed 2026-09-05). A variant splits any secured discount: "If available, Client will receive 50% of Designer discount and Designer will receive 50%" (agreement clause quoted via [Capella Kincheloe, Anatomy of an Interior Design Agreement 2.0](https://www.capellakincheloe.com/blog/anatomy-of-an-interior-design-agreement-2), accessed 2026-09-05).

**Flat/fixed design fee (procurement priced separately).** A single rate for design work, set by "experience, square footage, estimated time and budget," commonly bundled with cost-plus for the goods themselves ([Capella Kincheloe](https://www.capellakincheloe.com/blog/pricing-interior-design-services), accessed 2026-09-05; [Dakota Design Co](https://www.dakotadesigncompany.com/blog/how-to-price-your-interior-design-services), accessed 2026-09-05).

**Hourly.** Dakota Design Co: designers "provide a detailed scope and an estimate of hours, letting clients know upfront what they can expect to pay for their scope," and recommend hourly for designers just starting out who can reliably track time ([Dakota Design Co](https://www.dakotadesigncompany.com/blog/how-to-price-your-interior-design-services), accessed 2026-09-05). Studio Designer cites a range of "a firm principal might charge $175–$200 per hour (or more)," varying by staff seniority ([Studio Designer](https://www.studiodesigner.com/blog/pricing-structures-for-interior-designers/), accessed 2026-09-05).

**Percentage of project cost/budget.** Design fee set as a percentage of construction and/or furnishings spend — Kincheloe's example: "Final Budget: $61,392 x 20% = $12,278.40" ([Capella Kincheloe](https://www.capellakincheloe.com/blog/pricing-interior-design-services), accessed 2026-09-05). Dakota Design Co frames this as used "primarily [by] architects, general contractors, or designers handling new construction" ([Dakota Design Co](https://www.dakotadesigncompany.com/blog/how-to-price-your-interior-design-services), accessed 2026-09-05).

**Commission/markup-only (no separate design fee).** Dakota Design Co lists this as a distinct model but notes: they have "never worked with a client who bills this way" — i.e., it exists in taxonomy but is rare in their client base ([Dakota Design Co](https://www.dakotadesigncompany.com/blog/how-to-price-your-interior-design-services), accessed 2026-09-05).

**Hybrid.** The most commonly described real-world pattern: "Flat fee for design, then hourly for everything after that" or "Flat fee for design, ordering, and install, then hourly for everything else" ([Dakota Design Co](https://www.dakotadesigncompany.com/blog/how-to-price-your-interior-design-services), accessed 2026-09-05). Studio Designer separately describes phase-based fees breaking a project into "concept development, design execution, procurement, and installation" as billing stages ([Studio Designer](https://www.studiodesigner.com/blog/pricing-structures-for-interior-designers/), accessed 2026-09-05).

**Freight/receiving/install cost allocation is inconsistent across the industry.** Per an Interior Design Community survey of designer responses: "Answers from designers ranged from 10% processing fees to 25% flat rates to no markup at all, with the reasoning tied directly to each designer's business model" ([Interior Design Community, freight markup](https://interiordesigncommunity.com/freight-markup-interior-designers/), accessed 2026-09-05 — via search synthesis, not directly fetched). A "procurement fee — charged as a flat percentage of the total furniture budget, separate from any markup — is increasingly standard among mid-to-high-end design firms" per the same source. No source gave a single dominant convention for who contractually bears freight/receiving/install cost; multiple sources describe it as a contract-by-contract decision, not an industry default.

### Inferred
No survey source was found quantifying what share of practicing residential designers use each model (cost-plus vs. retail-minus vs. flat vs. hourly vs. percentage vs. hybrid). Repeated secondary-source claims that "most" studios in 2026 use a hybrid (flat design fee + cost-plus on FF&E) appear consistently across aggregator content (Vividly Built, Nubuor Designs, Roomwork.ai) but these are marketing/SEO content rather than primary surveys, so this report treats the claim as widely asserted but not survey-verified.

## 2. Typical markup / margin ranges

### Verified

**Overall markup-on-cost.** "The industry average product markup for interior designers is 35%, but the rate each designer applies varies greatly," with most designers charging 30–35% and some reaching 50–60%; the same source calls 25% "a lower-than-average markup rate" ([DesignFiles blog](https://blog.designfiles.co/how-should-i-markup-products/), accessed 2026-09-05).

**Markup-vs-margin distinction, explicitly worked.** Interior Design Community: "A piece that costs you $1,000 and sells for $1,600 carries a 60 percent markup. But the profit on that sale, $600, is only 37.5 percent of the $1,600 you actually collected" — i.e., "a 100% markup only yields 50% margin" ([Interior Design Community, profit margin](https://interiordesigncommunity.com/interior-design-profit-margin/), accessed 2026-09-05). The same piece reports a practitioner benchmark: "55–60% markup is basically a 30–35% margin," with a recommended target of 40% margin (noting that requires "serious vendor discounts"), and per-project variance — one designer "averaging about 30% to 40% on smaller projects and 20% to 30% on the larger ones," reasoning that smaller projects carry higher overhead per dollar.

**Sandra Funk (House of Funk / Interior Design Standard)** is reported discussing designers under-pricing at "10% or 20%, or even 30% markup," with the framing that "35% markup is break-even" (via search synthesis of [Interior Design Standard](https://interiordesignstandard.com/)-related podcast content, accessed 2026-09-05 — not independently fetched from a primary transcript).

**LuAnn Nigara / A Well-Designed Business** content is reported making the same markup-vs-margin point industry-wide: "a 35 percent markup, the default for most of this industry, doesn't produce a 35 percent margin. A 35 percent markup produces something closer to 26 percent" (via search synthesis associated with [luannnigara.com](https://luannnigara.com/), accessed 2026-09-05 — the specific episode page returned a 403 on direct fetch, so this quote is search-engine-sourced rather than page-verified).

**By category (markup-on-cost, mixed sourcing, treat as directional, not survey-grade):**
- General FF&E: "30 to 40% on cost" is cited as the "ideal markup" range ([search synthesis](https://businessofhome.com/), attributed generally to industry commentary, accessed 2026-09-05).
- Custom upholstery / bespoke pieces: "often support 60–100% markups because of the design consultation, specification management, and lead time investment involved" (via search synthesis, attributed to furniture-margin commentary, accessed 2026-09-05).
- Case goods and modular systems: "typically run 40–60%," but noted as "more competitive" because clients can price-check comparable retail items, making markup compression common in this category (same source cluster, accessed 2026-09-05).
- Lighting/accessories: repeatedly described as where designers "underutilise" margin opportunity, used to offset thinner margins elsewhere (same cluster).
- Soft goods (rugs, window treatments, bedding, pillows, accessories) collectively: cited as "10–15% of budget," with window treatments alone "consuming 3–5% of total budget for luxury fabrics and custom fabrication" (search synthesis, accessed 2026-09-05).
- Contractor/subcontractor management fees: "a 10 to 15% markup is standard," distinct from FF&E markup (search synthesis, accessed 2026-09-05).

**By firm size/positioning.** "Designers positioning themselves as luxury or boutique firms can justify markups of 40–50%, while those competing in value-conscious markets typically apply 20–30%" and "high-budget clients with project values exceeding $100,000 typically expect and accept standard to premium markups (35–50%)" (search synthesis, accessed 2026-09-05). Overhead-driven reasoning: "a solo designer operating from home with minimal marketing still faces $12,000 to $15,000 in annual overhead before earning a dollar," versus "firms with studio space and employees report monthly fixed costs of $17,000 or more" — used to argue that studio overhead, not just positioning, pushes markup upward for staffed firms (search synthesis, accessed 2026-09-05).

### Inferred
No source specifically addressed Midwest markets; all category/region claims above are national in scope. The category-by-category numbers above come from aggregator/SEO-style business content rather than a named, methodologically described survey (e.g., no ASID/IIDA compensation survey with sample size was located with markup-by-category breakdowns), so these ranges should be read as "commonly asserted in practitioner-adjacent business content," not as peer-reviewed or survey-weighted figures. Every number above nonetheless traces to an actual retrieved source; no number was invented for this report.

## 3. Disclosure norms

### Verified

**ASID Code of Ethics.** Fetched directly from the ASID chapter site: "Members shall fully disclose to a client all compensation that the member shall receive in connection with the project and shall not accept any form of undisclosed compensation from any person or firm with whom the member deals in connection with the project" ([ASID Code of Ethics](https://casd.asid.org/code-of-ethics), accessed 2026-09-05, reported as Section 3.3 in this fetch; a separate search-engine synthesis of the same page numbered it Section 3.4 — the section number could not be independently reconciled between the two retrievals, but the clause text is consistent across both).

**IIDA Code of Ethics.** Requires that members "reasonably inform the client of the scope and nature of the project involved, the interior design services to be performed, and the method of remuneration for those services" (search synthesis of IIDA chapter code-of-ethics pages, e.g. [IIDA-WI](https://iidawi.org/Code_of_Ethics) and [IIDA Oregon](https://www.iida-or.org/membership/iida-code-of-ethics), accessed 2026-09-05). This is a softer disclosure standard than ASID's — "method of remuneration" rather than "all compensation."

**State-level / legal.** California: "certified interior designers are required by law to provide a written contract as stated in CBPC Section 5807," with markup percentages and related costs (shipping, taxes, handling) expected to be documented in that written contract ([search synthesis](https://ccidc.org/consumer-items-of-interest/), accessed 2026-09-05). Virginia case law (both cases described as heard in Virginia, per the source): "Undisclosed markups on items procured for the client were found to be a breach of agreement because they were not expressly authorized," and "charging undisclosed markups, as well as having undisclosed rebate and commission arrangements with vendors, was found to be a violation of Virginia's Consumer Protection Act" ([Design Ink Co](https://www.designinkco.com/blog/interior-design-markup-disclosure), accessed 2026-09-05, citing underlying Virginia litigation reporting also indexed via [Virginia Business Litigation Blog](https://www.virginiabusinesslitigationlawyer.com/interior-designers-unauthorized-markups-may-be-grounds-for-legal-action/)).

**What counts as adequate disclosure language.** Design Ink Co is explicit that generic language is not enough: "'Industry-standard markup' is not considered sufficient disclosure," and that "Your written agreement governs, not common practice in the trade." The same source's four-model taxonomy for how designers phrase this in agreements: pure cost-plus (exact vendor invoice plus professional fees, full transparency); cost-plus with disclosed markup (trade cost plus an agreed, stated procurement fee); retail/resale model (buy at trade, sell at retail, profit margin not framed as "cost-plus"); fee-only (charge for time/expertise, discounts passed through or documented) ([Design Ink Co](https://www.designinkco.com/blog/interior-design-markup-disclosure), accessed 2026-09-05).

**Representative letter-of-agreement clauses (quoted verbatim, via [Capella Kincheloe, Anatomy of an Interior Design Agreement 2.0](https://www.capellakincheloe.com/blog/anatomy-of-an-interior-design-agreement-2), accessed 2026-09-05):**
1. Discount-sharing / markup clause: *"If available, Client will receive 50% of Designer discount and Designer will receive 50%."*
2. Deposit clause: *"Upon signing this Agreement, Designer shall receive a non refundable initial advance of $rate, which constitutes the minimum fee due Designer for Design Services."*
3. Purchasing/procurement fee clause: *"Purchasing is billed at a rate of $rate per hour. Full deposit is required for each item and only upon receipt of deposit Designer will place order."*

A fourth, from Design Ink Co's recommended-language example (distinct source, same theme): *"Client will be charged the net cost plus a procurement fee of X% on approved furnishings. Trade discounts, if any, are retained by the designer as part of studio compensation."*

**Documentation obligations follow through the relationship**, not just at signing: "if your contract says you'll share supplier invoices, you must share them. Failing to provide promised documentation can escalate disputes significantly" ([Design Ink Co](https://www.designinkco.com/blog/interior-design-markup-disclosure), accessed 2026-09-05).

### Inferred
No state besides California surfaced a specific statutory disclosure requirement in this research pass; the legal exposure for undisclosed markup elsewhere appears to run through general contract/consumer-protection law (breach of agreement, deceptive trade practices) rather than an interior-design-specific licensing statute. This is an inference from the pattern across sources, not a confirmed 50-state survey.

## 4. What the client sees

### Verified

**Itemization is the dominant recommended practice, not a single "investment" number.** Per synthesis of proposal-template guidance: "Presenting one total figure without itemization increases rejection risk and blocks selective approval, which is why professional interior design proposals typically include detailed breakdowns rather than single numbers," with recommended sections including "Design Scope of Work, Furnishings & Sourced Items, Design Selections Summary, Change Order Log, Line-Item Quote, Project Cost Summary, Payment Schedule, and Terms & Conditions" (search synthesis of proposal-template sources including [Proposify](https://www.proposify.com/proposal-templates/interior-design-proposal-template) and [PandaDoc](https://www.pandadoc.com/interior-design-proposal-template/), accessed 2026-09-05).

**Trade/net cost is generally not shown to the client**, even by designers who describe themselves as transparent: "Transparency in pricing doesn't mean passing along your trade pricing, disclosing proprietary to-the-trade vendor information, or explaining how you came to your hourly rate" ([search synthesis](https://www.dakotadesigncompany.com/blog/how-to-share-interior-design-pricing-with-prospects), accessed 2026-09-05). Some designers instead disclose the *existence* of a markup or discount-share without showing the underlying trade invoice, per the discount-sharing clause pattern in Section 3 above.

**Document-type taxonomy, per software vendor documentation (Ivy/Houzz Pro):**
- *Proposal*: "the starting point for projects," a "flexible document ... meant to show estimated costs," client-facing, pricing shown before vendor engagement.
- *Purchase Order*: created once a proposal is approved and a deposit collected, used to "begin the purchasing process and receive the finalized cost from your vendor"; vendor-facing only — "all of your vendor-facing product details, such as your product title, vendor, SKU, manufacture, and vendor description will all transfer over to the purchase order automatically," and the platform "automatically separates what is client facing and vendor facing."
- *Invoice*: used for final billing reflecting actual costs; if vendor cost changes between proposal and PO stages, the guidance is to "Create an invoice for the item from the original proposal and change the cost on the invoice."
(All quotes: [Ivy's Guide to Purchase Orders](https://www.ivy.co/designers/magazine/ivy-news/ivys-guide-to-purchase-orders/), accessed 2026-09-05.)

**Deposit collection is tied to the Proposal stage, not the PO or Invoice**: "Ivy allows you to collect a deposit on proposals, and we highly recommend that you collect as much money up front before paying your vendor" (same source, accessed 2026-09-05).

**Studio Designer's help-center documentation** (per search-result snippets, direct fetch blocked by TLS/cert errors on two attempts) describes an equivalent flow: designers enter a "Purchase Cost," which "can be copied to the amount you want to charge your client (Selling Price)," inside a "Creating Proposals" workflow described as "a document you can send to clients to get their approval as well as deposit payment on items" ([Studio Designer Help Center listings](https://help.studiodesigner.com/s/article/Creating-Proposals), accessed 2026-09-05, via search snippet only — direct page fetch failed with a certificate error, noted here per instructions to note blocked sources and move on).

**Houzz Pro / Ivy platform capability**: "create and send beautifully branded and formatted proposals," "easily send invoices to your clients for payment and collect deposits upfront on any invoice or proposal," and "convert proposals to purchase orders, populate addresses from vendors, track the status of items ordered," with sync to QuickBooks Online for "every Estimate Proposal, Invoice, Purchase Order, and expense" ([Houzz Pro for Ivy](https://pro.houzz.com/for-pros/ivy); [Houzz Pro Purchase Order template guide](https://www.houzz.com/pro-learn/blog/startup-guide-interior-design-how-to-make-purchase-order-with-template), accessed 2026-09-05).

### Inferred
Whether a given studio shows itemized line pricing, room subtotals, or a single collapsed "investment" figure appears to be a studio-level choice rather than an industry norm — practitioner guidance favors itemization for approval psychology, but no source quantified what fraction of studios actually present a single number to clients. The naming convention "investment" itself (as opposed to "price" or "cost") appears in proposal-template marketing copy but no primary source in this research explained its adoption rate or origin with a citation-worthy claim.

## 5. How designers think about the number

### Verified

**Multiple simultaneous mental frames, often not reconciled by the designer.** The clearest documented confusion is markup-on-cost vs. margin-on-price: Interior Design Community frames the practical failure mode as designers setting a markup percentage without checking what margin it actually produces, illustrating that a "100% markup only yields 50% margin," and recommends "calculating a 'blended target' across the whole project rather than forcing uniform line-item markups" so that "lower-margin vendor pieces" can be offset "against categories where your margin runs higher: lighting, accessories, custom upholstery, window treatments" ([Interior Design Community](https://interiordesigncommunity.com/interior-design-profit-margin/), accessed 2026-09-05).

**Framing markup as earned professional labor, not pure profit-taking.** DesignFiles' recommended client-facing talking points: pricing "covers your time spent sourcing, ordering, tracking, and resolving any issues with that product," and designers are coached to note that clients "might pay a higher price on their own" at retail, so "even with a markup, they may end up paying less or around the market rate" — i.e., framing the number relative to retail/MSRP rather than relative to the designer's own cost ([DesignFiles](https://blog.designfiles.co/how-should-i-markup-products/), accessed 2026-09-05).

**"What the client will accept" as an explicit variable.** Segmentation by client budget and market positioning recurs across sources: designers are advised that luxury/boutique positioning "can justify markups of 40–50%," value-conscious markets "typically apply 20–30%," and clients on projects "exceeding $100,000 typically expect and accept standard to premium markups (35–50%)" — i.e., acceptance threshold, not just cost-recovery math, sets the number in practice (search synthesis, accessed 2026-09-05).

**Coaching content explicitly separates "pricing structure" from "markup-setting" as two different decisions.** Dakota Design Co's public blog content addresses which billing *model* to use (hourly/flat/percentage/hybrid) but explicitly defers markup-percentage-setting to a separate paid workshop: "The Pricing Playbook focuses on choosing the right pricing structure for your design services — not on setting markup percentages for furnishings," directing designers elsewhere for "markup strategies, pricing for profitability, and selling to-the-trade furnishings" ([Dakota Design Co](https://www.dakotadesigncompany.com/blog/how-to-price-your-interior-design-services), accessed 2026-09-05). This indicates the "which mental model" question (model choice) and "what's the number" question (markup-setting) are treated by at least one major practitioner-educator as two distinct skills taught separately.

**Whole-project/blended thinking over per-line-item uniformity** recurs as an explicit recommendation, not just a description: designers reporting "averaging about 30% to 40% on smaller projects and 20% to 30% on the larger ones" are cited as reasoning about overhead recovery per project rather than per item, because "smaller projects carry higher overhead per dollar, requiring higher percentages to remain profitable, while larger projects can sustain lower percentages through volume" ([Interior Design Community](https://interiordesigncommunity.com/interior-design-profit-margin/), accessed 2026-09-05).

### Inferred
Direct, freely accessible primary-source practitioner writing from Capella Kincheloe, Sandra Funk, and LuAnn Nigara on this exact question was partially paywalled or blocked in this research pass (Business of Home's "Dictionary of Markups" is subscriber-only; a specific LuAnn Nigara episode page returned HTTP 403). Where this report cites those voices, it relies on search-engine synthesis of indexed page content rather than a directly fetched, fully verifiable primary page — flagged inline above at each such citation. The consistent throughline across everything that *was* independently fetched (Capella Kincheloe's own site, Dakota Design Co's own site, Design Ink Co, Interior Design Community, DesignFiles, Ivy) is that designers are coached to reason in blended/whole-project margin terms while communicating in cost-plus or retail-minus language to clients — i.e., the internal mental model and the client-facing language are treated by these educators as deliberately different registers.
