# Memo — seat 4, the practicing designer

Leah Hartwell · Middle West Studio, Madison · 10 September 2026

I have sixteen jobs open. The Okonkwos want their agreement this morning and I
have a site visit at ten and another at one. I ran the same thirteen steps
against the room as it stands and against all four proposed directions. Below
is what happened, in my hands, on the Okonkwo house — design services agreement.

---

## What today's room costs me

Fifty-four presses, eighteen scrolls, fifteen times my attention had to move
from one part of the screen to another, and about seventeen minutes. Seventeen
minutes is not the problem. The problem is that I was unsure of where I stood
seventeen separate times, and four of those were about whether my work was
safe.

The room asks me to read three different tallies of my own trouble. The bar at
the top says *2 of 9 parts need attention*. The right-hand margin says the same
words again. Underneath that, two more sentences tell me things that also stop
me from sending — *This agreement names no fee* and *Link a client with an email
address* — and neither of those is counted in the two. So the room can tell me
*0 of 9 parts need attention* while refusing to let me send at all. I saw
exactly that on the retainer plate. That is not a small thing. That is the room
telling me I am finished when I am not.

Then the count moves and nothing says why. I filled in the three role rates,
left the ceiling for a moment, and a third row lit up while my hands were in a
dollar field. I set the retainer and the count went down again. Both times I
had to stop, scroll back up the list, and hunt for the row whose small grey
words had changed. Nothing spoke. The only line on that page that ever
announces anything is the one that says my changes were saved.

And the paper — the reason any of this exists — is a column two hundred and
eighty points wide. The title of the Okonkwo agreement wraps onto three lines
inside it. I can see that words are present. I cannot read a paragraph, I
cannot judge a rate card, and I certainly cannot tell whether the Terms
paragraph reads well to a homeowner. Kody is right that it is too thin. He is
right for a bigger reason than he says: it is too thin to do the job it is
there for, so I stop using it and press *Preview client copy* instead — which
opens a second copy of the same document, six hundred and forty wide, directly
on top of the first, cut off at the bottom of my screen and scrolling inside
itself. Two copies of one paper on one screen, one hiding the other. That is
the moment the room stopped feeling like a desk.

Two more things I want on the record before the rankings.

The first: on my laptop I cannot send this agreement. The only way into the
send is a word in the bar at the top of the room, and that word is not there
below a fairly wide screen. Not smaller. Not moved. Not there. I write
agreements on a thirteen-inch laptop at a client's dining table more often than
I write them at my desk, and on that machine the room has no ending.

The second: the act that throws away every part I have written is a plain box,
the same size and weight as *Preview client copy*, sitting second from the left
in a row of three, exactly where a hand lands. One press. No sentence, no
question, nothing to take back. I did not press it, and I was careful not to,
and being careful not to press a button is not a thing a working room should
ask of me.

## What I would give up, and what I would not

Give up gladly: the third column; the box that says *Saved*; the *Preview* act
as a separate errand, **if** the paper is genuinely the page; the *Client
account* block with its own heading and its own explanatory sentence; the
duplicate count in the top bar; the word *rename* being buried in a menu.

Not give up, at any price:

- **Seeing the client's copy while I type, not after.** This is the whole
  argument. I write a sentence for Dave Okonkwo and I need to see the sentence
  he reads, in his type, at his measure, in the same second. Every direction
  that makes me finish, close something, and then look is a direction that will
  have me writing agreements in Word again by Christmas.
- **A full read of the whole paper before I send.** Not a preview act — a
  reading. I want one route that shows me the paper end to end at its own
  proper width, and I want to take it as a last check, not as a way of seeing
  what I just typed.
- **The money as a form, not as prose.** Three roles, three rates, a ceiling, a
  retainer. Typed in fields, printed as a schedule. Nobody should be writing
  `$185.00 / hour` into a paragraph.
- **Being able to take a part out.** Including Exclusions. Half my agreements
  do not need half of these.
- **Moving a part without a mouse**, and being told plainly that it moved.
- **The paper never printing a nought.** *Not yet set* is right and I would
  fight for it.

## On the four directions

**D, the galley,** is the one I would work in. The printed part stays printed
and its pencil unfolds underneath it — so I never lose the sentence I am
rewriting, which is exactly what I lose in A. The note about the missing fee
prints in the margin beside the seam where the fee would print, which is where
my eye already is. Reorder is *Move up* and *Move down* on the part's own head,
which is the single biggest saving in the whole exercise: today it took me ten
presses to get the Concept fee up out of the basement, and the little menu shut
after every one of them.

**A, the paper is the page,** is nearly as good and faster — thirty-three
presses against today's fifty-four, and only three moments of doubt in the whole
run. Its risk is that selecting a part *replaces* the printed part with its
editor. That is the one trade I do not want, and the money editor living in the
margin on a wide screen but full-width on a phone means the same job has two
different homes.

**B, Kody's hunch,** solves the right problem — the paper becomes the page, and
the first screen I see is the document — but it puts a door between me and every
single edit. Open, edit, close. Fourteen minutes and thirteen changes of place.
And on a phone the paper is gone entirely while I work, which is today's
blindness with a nicer frame. I would take B over today without hesitating. I
would not take it over D or A.

**C** costs me fifty-three presses to today's fifty-four. It is a tidier room
with the same trouble in it.

## Ranking A–D, one sentence each

1. **D · the galley** — the printed part stays printed while I write underneath it, the note about the missing fee sits beside the seam where the fee would print, and moving a part is two presses on its own head rather than five trips through a menu that shuts each time.
2. **A · the paper is the page** — the fastest run by a distance and the fewest moments of doubt, and I would rank it first if selecting a part did not take the printed sentence away from me while I rewrite it.
3. **B · the builder as a drawer** — it gets the big thing right, that the document is the page, but it puts a door between me and every single edit and it takes the paper away completely on a phone.
4. **C · two panes** — a tidier room with almost exactly today's cost in presses, and the rate card ends up in a narrower measure than the one I am already complaining about.

## What the specimen must show to change my mind

- **D, at a laptop width.** Show me the galley with the two margins collapsed and the rate card unfolded beneath its own printed form. If the marginal note goes inline and the outline goes away at 1024 as well as 390, D has lost the thing that won it and A moves to first.
- **A, keeping the printed sentence.** Show me a clause selected on the paper where the printed paragraph is still legible while I type — not replaced by a box. If A can do that, A is first and it is not close.
- **A, the money at three widths.** The Okonkwo rate card — Principal $185.00, Designer $140.00, Assistant $85.00 — edited at 1440, 1024 and 390, with the ceiling still unset and the sentence about it visible in all three. One home for that job, not two.
- **B, the paper behind the drawer.** Show me the drawer open on Services at 1440 with the *Services* section of the paper visibly beside it, scrolled to, updating. Not "the paper updates" — the part I am touching, in view. That moves B above C and possibly above A.
- **Any of them, the tally.** Show me the moment the retainer is set: the count moving from 3 to 2 *and* a sentence naming what cleared, in a place I am already looking. That is P1 for me in every direction and it does not change my ranking — it changes whether I would use any of them.
- **Any of them, at the dining table.** Show me the send act on the page at 1024. Today it does not exist there, and no amount of better composing fixes an agreement I cannot send.
