# The Invoice, Standing Alone — adversarial review

T3 · 2026-09-06 · Adversarial reviewer (Opus, separate context). Read-only on repo code.
Attacks `design/01-directions.md` (T1) and `architecture/02-system-design.md` (T2) against the code they cite.
Rulings K1–K3 are fixed and are not under review. Every finding is reported; no severity filter applied.

---

## 1 · Verdict

The architecture is unusually well-grounded — twenty of its twenty-one corrections hold against the files they cite, and its central judgement (a sibling `claim_invoice_link_checkout_attempt` rather than a signature change to `claim_invoice_checkout_attempt`) is the right one, because it leaves the pinned 5-arg signature literal at `supabase/tests/billing/invoice_checkout_integrity_test.sql:738,743` untouched. But the `payer_id`-nullable path is traced only as far as the claim RPC: it stops before three places that will break or silently weaken on a NULL payer — `create-checkout-session/index.ts:853,973` throws on a falsy `payer_id`, `stripe-webhook/invoice-checkout-integrity.ts:6,101,107` loses its entire payer term with nothing replacing it, and `claim_invoice_checkout_attempt` (00428:294-296) raises on an actor change *before* the supersede branch, with no sweep anywhere in the repo to clear a stale attempt — so one stranger with the link can permanently block the household from paying its own invoice. The design directions are strong and B is the right pick, but two of their load-bearing instruments are wrong about the code: `moneyInWords` does not spell money in words and silently rounds cents away, and the "send null card bps" ruling would disable card payment for every studio without a `studio_billing_settings` row, which is every studio by default.

**Recommendation: do not ship W1 as written.** Fix M1–M3, S1, S5, G1, G2, G5 first; the rest are correctable in the deck or in review.

## 3 · Answers to the design lead's six questions

**Q1 — null vs 300 for the card rate.** Send **300**, always; delete the null/held branch. This is not a judgement call — the code has already made it. `claim_invoice_checkout_attempt` coalesces the studio bps to 300 immediately before computing the surcharge it will actually charge (`00428:250-253`), and `get_invoice_payment_options` returns `coalesce(v_bps, 300)` with a COMMENT that says "defaulting to {300, null} when the studio has no settings row." The premise that 300 "quotes a studio a rate it never set" is backwards: the `card_surcharge_bps` CHECK bounds the value at 0–300 with 300 as the column default, so an unconfigured studio has accepted the platform maximum by not lowering it. And the practical stakes are severe: `studio_billing_settings` rows are written only by a studio admin — there is no backfill, no trigger, no seed — so the overwhelming majority of studios have no row, and a page that holds the card act whenever the rate is "unknown" would disable card payment for almost every invoice Patina sends. The deleted page's straddle existed only because `get_invoice_payment_options` was a second round trip that could be in flight; the standalone page renders from one payload and has no in-flight window. Keep `—` for nothing.

**Q2 — does the token print?** Accept it, and say so on the sheet. Browsers stamp the URL into the print header by default, no stylesheet can suppress it, and the two alternatives are both worse: a "printable copy" at a cookie-resolved token-free URL breaks the one property that makes this page work (no account, any device, forwardable), and a visible warning line on a studio's invoice is chrome in the exact place the design has worked hardest to keep clean. The honest mitigation is a print-only line in the colophon, in the same quiet register as the rest: *"This sheet carries a payment link. Treat it like a check."* One line, at the foot, below the studio's own name — it belongs to the studio's voice rather than to a security banner. Note that this consideration also argues *against* S10's acceptance of the token in Stripe's logs: if the link is worth warning a bookkeeper about, it is worth keeping out of a third party's retained exports, and the one-time-nonce return URL becomes cheap by comparison.

**Q3 — what does the surcharge do on paper?** The ruling is right and should stand: an unpaid sheet prints **no** fee and **no** Total to pay. A fee contingent on a choice the reader has not made is not a debt, and the 00428 invariant that `amount_cents` stays the pure invoice-applied balance is the accounting fact the printed sheet should carry — a bookkeeper filing this needs the invoice's number, not the till's. The $9,125-on-paper / $9,398.75-at-checkout divergence the question worries about is not a discrepancy; it is the difference between what is owed and what a particular payment method costs, and the design already refuses to fold them together everywhere else. Do **not** add the three-total table to print: it would put a card-vs-check comparison into the artifact most likely to be scanned into an accounting inbox, where it reads as a price list. A paid sheet printing the charged figure on the payment row is correct and matches the `payments[].surcharge_cents` the payload carries.

**Q4 — void: dead, or withdrawn?** Keep **dead**, per K2 — but the question is less interesting than the case it exposes. The softer state genuinely leaks nothing (the token is unguessable, so naming the studio to its holder tells them what they already knew), and I would happily rule either way on aesthetics. What forces the answer is M10: an invoice can be voided while a guest's Checkout session is open, and if the guest then pays, the charge is frozen at `requires_refund`, no receipt is sent, and the bare dead link is what they see after being charged. So the resolver needs a *third* behaviour regardless of this ruling — alive-but-unpayable when a void invoice carries a `pending` or `requires_refund` payment. Build that state first; then "withdrawn by {studio}" is a two-line variation on a sheet that already exists, and Kody can rule on it with something in front of him. Until then, dead.

**Q5 — whose name goes on the check?** The **studio** is the payee; the **designer** is the person. Those are different roles and the copy should stop mixing them: the remit-to block is addressed to `studio.name` (which `resolve_studio_identity` resolves off `invoices.studio_id`, so it is right for a two-studio designer and for a houseless studio invoice), and the notify act names `designer_display_name` because a person, not an entity, is going to mark it received. So: *"Mail your check to Quist Interiors"* over the address, *"Write Invoice No. 4 on the memo line,"* and *"Let Nora know a check is coming."* The rule the design lead could not find in code is implicit in the payload's own shape — `studio` carries `name`/`logo_url`/`website` (an entity's letterhead) while `designer_display_name` is a bare human name. One caveat: `check_remit_to` (00428) is free text set by a studio admin and is NULL for most studios today, so the fallback string is the common case — use the tested `CHECK_REMIT_FALLBACK`, "Contact your designer for mailing details," which already resolves this exact ambiguity by naming the person and not the entity.

**Q6 — is ACH-first a nudge Patina is entitled to make?** Order is not the problem; the **claim** is. Nothing in Vision forbids putting a route first, and there is a legitimate non-self-interested reason to lead with ACH: it is the cheapest of the two routes that settle immediately, and check — genuinely cheapest for the homeowner — costs them a stamp, a trip, and three weeks of the designer chasing it. What Patina is *not* entitled to is the sentence "the lowest fee of the three," which is simply false with check on the list (G3), or "the fee is what the rail costs," which over-claims cost recovery on card (G4). Fix those two strings and the ordering defends itself on the page: three routes, three real totals visible at once, cheapest-to-the-homeowner plainly marked $0 — the reader can see the nudge and overrule it in one click. That is the strongest available reading of §4's "no hidden fees," and it is stronger than reordering would be. Keep ACH first and pre-selected; tell the truth beside it.

## 4 · The alternative payer design I considered

**The proposal:** `payer_id` widened to nullable behind `CHECK (payer_id IS NOT NULL OR invoice_link_id IS NOT NULL)`, a per-link Stripe customer on `invoice_links.stripe_customer_id`, and a sibling `claim_invoice_link_checkout_attempt`.

**The alternative I weighed:** keep `payer_id NOT NULL` and give the link a **synthetic payer** — a `profiles` row per payer-less household, minted by the resolver, carrying the `designer_clients.client_email`/`client_name` and its own `stripe_customer_id`. Every existing assertion then holds verbatim: `claim_invoice_checkout_attempt` unchanged, `finalize`/`recover` identity checks keep a real term, `assertInvoiceSessionIdentity` untouched, `invoice-checkout-integrity.ts` untouched, `recorded_by` non-null, generated types unchanged, the pinned test signature unchanged. It is a much smaller diff and it preserves every invariant this review spent its time checking. A third option — a `guest_payers` table with its own FK — I discarded early: it is the proposal's shape with an extra join and no gain, since `invoice_links` already has exactly the right cardinality.

**Why I nonetheless side with the proposal, with reservations.** The synthetic-payer route founders on `profiles.id PRIMARY KEY REFERENCES auth.users(id)` (`00013:13`): you cannot mint a profile without minting an `auth.users` row, and `handle_new_user` (`00013:72-88`) is its only writer. So "synthetic payer" means **creating a real Supabase auth account for a homeowner who never asked for one**, silently, at the moment they click Pay. That is an account the person did not consent to, that can receive password-reset mail, that changes what `designer_clients` means, and that would make `client-invite`'s careful "relabel an existing profile, never insert one" behaviour (`client-invite/index.ts:273-280`) a lie. It also inverts Vision §4 — the homeowner would very much notice Patina. T2's note 3 reached the same conclusion and I confirmed each link of its chain independently (`00018:2-5` dropping `designer_clients.client_id NOT NULL`, `00013:13`, `00013:72-88`, `00555:1146-1149`); it is correct.

**My verdict: the proposal is right, but it is under-specified by roughly three files.** Widening `payer_id` is not the change — the change is that *every consumer of payer identity now has a second shape to handle*, and the design tracked it into the claim RPC and stopped. M1 (the driver throws on a falsy payer), M2 (the webhook's independent identity check silently loses its only payer term), M3 (the claim raises rather than supersedes on an actor change, with no sweep to recover) and M4 (finalize/recover degenerate to vacuous) are all the same omission wearing four hats. Fix them by making `invoice_link_id` a **first-class identity term everywhere `payer_id` is one** — the attempt table, the Stripe metadata, `assertInvoiceSessionIdentity`, `invoice-checkout-integrity.ts`, `finalize`, `recover` — rather than a nullable column that only the claim function knows about. Done that way the CHECK constraint becomes a real discriminated union and the money invariants survive; done as written, the guest rail settles on `stripe_customer_id` alone and nobody notices until an incident.

One smaller reservation: `ALTER COLUMN payer_id DROP NOT NULL` is genuinely irreversible once a link attempt exists, as §12 admits. That is acceptable for a widening — but it means M1–M4 must be right the *first* time, which is the argument for putting W1 through a second reviewer after the fixes, not for re-litigating the shape.
