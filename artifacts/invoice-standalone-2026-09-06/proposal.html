1 · The ask

An invoice should not need an account.

Kody asked for one thing with three parts: invoices must be stand-alone pages, viewable with just a link and no account, with the payment method chosen on the page and the real total to pay moving live as the method toggles. He also asked us to go and look at what the invoice page was before the client-page cutover, assemble a team to design and architect the invoicing system around it, and present it as a deck and a full-fidelity mockup.

Everything after this paragraph is that work. The design is settled, the architecture is verified line by line against the code it cites, an adversarial reviewer attacked both and found forty-nine things, and the deck ends with four rulings only Kody can make. The build is a separate request.

The three rulings that bind everything below

#Ruling
K1One pay surface. The stand-alone page is the invoice, for everyone. Email links, the letterbox, iOS and Stripe return URLs all land there. The letterbox becomes a ledger line and an "Open the invoice" act; settling in place is retired. This supersedes R135's clause "the acts never leave the page — including the toll," and is logged as a new R-number when built.
K2Full invoice; the link lives with the invoice. Anyone holding the link sees letterhead, lines, totals, memo and payments, and can pay the balance. It is valid while the invoice is open, stays as a receipt after payment, and dies on void. The designer can regenerate it — revoke and reissue — from the folio.
K3The deck carries design, architecture and delivery — migration numbers, deploy order, gates and rollback — so that "ship it" can follow directly from it.

2 · The vision test

Four questions, before a line is written.

Every feature answers four questions before it is built: which surface, which studio moment, which stream, which promise. This one answers all four without strain.

SurfaceThe Document. The client page is the Document's homeowner face (R135), and /pay/<token> is that same face with the account taken out of the way. It is not a fourth surface.
Studio momentThe studio bills a homeowner who has never signed into anything. Today that homeowner's bill arrives, opens a sign-in wall, and turns into a phone call to the designer. The studio's second hire spends the afternoon on it.
StreamBoth. The margin stream directly — this is the till, and it is currently locked behind a password the payer does not have. The subscription floor indirectly: a studio whose bills get paid without a support thread stays.
PromiseVision §4, twice. No hidden fees: the page shows all three arrived-at totals at once, before the reader touches anything, and never folds a fee into a balance. You won't notice Patina: the letterhead is the studio's, the person named is the designer, and Patina is one line at the foot of the sheet.

It passes on all four. The one place it presses on the promise is the host — an account-less homeowner is sent to client.patina.cloud, a name written for the account holder they are not. That was weighed and ruled: the host stands, because K2 makes these URLs permanent and a later move to a prettier domain would break every link already in the wild. It is recorded as a decision in §12, not left as an omission.

3 · What the page was, what it is now

The parts were right. The order was wrong, and then it was deleted.

There was a real invoice page, and it did nearly everything this program asks for — except stand alone. It was deleted two days ago, on 4 September, as part of the client-page cutover. We recovered it from git and read it top to bottom.

What the cutover did

The stand-alone route is gone. /invoices/<id> is now 308-folded by middleware to /?invoice=<id>#letterbox (middleware.ts:264-290, mapping at lib/retired-routes.ts:63,145-152). Only /invoices/[invoiceId]/print survives as a real route, and it is still session-gated.

The chooser survived near-verbatim into components/threshold/payment-method-chooser.tsx — same three options, same arithmetic, same check panel. What did not survive is the presentation of the money: the fee row and "Total to pay" were replaced by a single sentence at settlement.tsx:183-192 — "You will be charged $9,398.75 — the balance and a $273.75 processing fee." The maths is byte-identical; the table is gone.

The wall a homeowner hits today

Every invoice letter Patina sends points at ${CLIENT_PORTAL_URL}/invoices/<id> — invoice-send:259, invoice-reminders:353, and both webhook letters at stripe-webhook:418,517. That path folds to the letterbox, and / is explicitly not in the public list (middleware.ts:148-152). A homeowner without an account is bounced to /auth/signin?callbackUrl=… and stops there. Every invoice read in the database is authenticated-only RLS (00178, 00571); create-checkout-session runs verify_jwt=true and proves the caller is invoice.client_id. There is no token, no anon policy, and no public invoice surface anywhere in the product.

4 · Three directions, and the pick

Where the pay decision sits relative to the record.

Three directions, one axis. At the foot of the letter, as a remittance slip. Beside it, in a money column. In front of it, as a counter. All three share the paper, the faces, the arithmetic and the nine states; they differ in structure, in what the typography argues, and in what they cost to build.

A · The Remittance

A studio letter on Document paper with a remittance slip at its foot. Below a dotted perforation reading Detach and return with your payment sits the only bordered block on the sheet: the three ways to pay, the amount, and the act. Because there is exactly one "total to pay" figure anywhere on the page, the toggle has nowhere to be ambiguous — choosing a method re-inks the stub itself. Playfair carries the letterhead and one other thing, the slip's amount; the moment your eye crosses the perforation the type changes register from letter to instrument. Its cost is honest: the fee is below the fold, and on a phone the slip is two screens down.

B · The Statement

The deleted page's bones, re-set on Document paper, with the chooser moved into a money column so the toggle and the number it moves sit forty pixels apart. Two measures on desktop: the record on the left — lines, memo, payments — and a standing column on the right holding the figures, the chooser, the totals stack, the fee row, Total to pay and the act. One column of the eye, top to bottom: what you owe → how you'd like to pay → what that comes to → the act. The column is not a card. It has no border, no fill, no shadow — a single hairline rule separates it from the record, the way a ledger separates its money column from its narrative, and that rule is the only structural device on the page.

C · The Counter

Pay first. The three ways to pay, each carrying the whole figure you would actually pay by that route, are the first thing on the page; the invoice unfolds beneath as the record. Selection is a change of voice, not a border — the chosen row's total promotes from mono to Playfair and the other two stay mono. On a paid invoice the counter is not disabled, it is gone, and the record stands alone as a receipt. Its cost, stated plainly by its own author: it asks for a payment decision before showing what is being bought, which demotes the record — and the record is the relationship.

The pick: B, with two borrowings

Build B, The Statement. Three reasons, in order of weight.

  1. It fixes the one real defect in the page Kody asked us to look at. The old page put the entire line-item list between the chooser and the number it moved. Kody's ask centres precisely on that mechanic. B is the only direction where the motion happens inside a single column of the eye, and it gets there by moving components that already exist rather than inventing an object.
  2. It honours K2 without ranking the record against the money. A link-holder who came to read the bill and one who came to settle it are served by the same first viewport, side by side. A wins on beauty by putting the till two screens down; C wins on speed by demoting the record. B refuses the trade — which is what Vision §4 asks for.
  3. It is the cheapest and least risky. The chooser, the totals stack, the surcharge arithmetic and every state's copy already exist in the repo.

Borrowed from C: all three arrived-at totals on the chooser rows, so a reader sees $9,130.00 / $9,398.75 / $9,125.00 at once. It costs one span per row and converts "no hidden fees" from a claim into something the reader can see without touching anything. B keeps its single moving Total to pay as the promoted figure; the three row totals never move, and their stillness is what makes the moving one legible.

Borrowed from A: the studio-first letterhead — which does trust-work B's statement band does not — and the print-only colophon line, "This sheet carries a payment link. Treat it like a check."

What the review corrected in the design

The adversarial reviewer found five design defects that were load-bearing. All five are folded into the mockup and the architecture.

#The defectThe correction
G1No spelled-out money. All three directions built a standing sentence — "Nine thousand one hundred twenty-five dollars stands due…" — on moneyInWords. That instrument does not spell money in words. Its body is Intl.NumberFormat(… maximumFractionDigits: 0): it returns "$9,125" and rounds the cents away.moneyInWords is banned from this page. Every figure is formatCurrency, including inside accessible names. On a page whose purpose is to charge an exact figure, a rounding formatter on the payable amount is a money defect.
G2The fixture arithmetic. The design carried an $18,250 invoice total. The fixture pins only the balance — $9,125 due 15 August; the $61,400-of-$85,000 pair beside it in the brief is the project agreement, not the invoice. The design's own "$1,520 gap" was an artefact of a made-up total.Total is the named line sum: $16,730.00. Received $7,605.00. $16,730 − $7,605 = $9,125.00, the canonical balance. No invented rooms, no elision, no rounded total. Composing all three named amounts onto Invoice No. 4 is the mockup's own arrangement, and this deck says so.
G3"The lowest fee of the three" is false. Mail a check is $0. The string was inherited from the deleted chooser's Preferred · lowest fee chip, and it survived into a direction that shows all three totals side by side — so the page would state a falsehood the reader could disprove one row down.The claim is dropped. The three figures do the work: + $5.00 beside + $273.75 beside No fee.
G4The fee sentences over-claimed. "The fee is what the payment rail costs; Quist Interiors doesn't add to it" asserts exact cost recovery. Card is a per-studio rate defaulting to 300 bps; Stripe's US card price is 2.9% + $0.30, which on the charged total of $9,398.75 is $272.86 — close to the 300 bps surcharge of $273.75, but not equal to it. A studio set below 300, or a bank transfer at 0.8% capped at $5.00 against Stripe's own ACH pricing, diverges further. And "rail" is payments jargon a homeowner will not read.Card: "This covers what card processing costs." Bank transfer: "Bank transfer costs the least to process." Check: "No fee." No equation, no "doesn't add to it," no "rail."
G5The card rate is never unknown. All three directions designed an em-dash "rate unknown" state that held the act. But claim_invoice_checkout_attempt does coalesce(v_card_bps, 300) immediately before charging (00428:254-257), and studio_billing_settings rows are written only by a studio admin — no backfill, no trigger — so most studios have no row. The held state would have disabled card payment across the board.card_surcharge_bps is always the coalesced integer, 300 by default. The em-dash and the held act are deleted everywhere. Similarly check_remit_to is NULL for the same majority, so the tested CHECK_REMIT_FALLBACK — "Contact your designer for mailing details" — is the common case, not an edge.

Two more design rulings fell out of the same pass. The "no payer" state is gone (G7): the link-payer path is built, so the payer's existence stops being a UI concept and the check-only variant is deleted. And the recommendation's claim that the guest sheet and the designer's folio are "the same document" was dropped (G6) — status is said on this page and coloured in the folio, and until the folio's colour is cleaned up, the sheets differ in exactly the dimension the design changed. That cleanup is a follow-up, not folded in.

6 · The toggle and the total

Three things move. Three things hold still.

This is the mechanic Kody asked for, and it is the whole reason direction B won: the radio and the number it changes sit forty pixels apart, inside one column of the eye. Selecting a row moves exactly three things and nothing else on the page shifts.

The payee rule the design could not find in code is implicit in the payload's own shape: studio carries a name, a logo and a website — an entity's letterhead — while designer_display_name is a bare human name. So the studio is the payee on the envelope, and the designer is the person you tell. That is right for a two-studio designer and for a houseless studio invoice alike.

ACH stays first and pre-selected. Nothing in the vision forbids putting a route first, and there is a non-self-interested reason to lead with it: it is the cheaper of the two routes that settle immediately, and a check costs the homeowner a stamp and the designer three weeks of chasing. What Patina is not entitled to is a false claim beside it — which is why G3 and G4 were fixed rather than argued. With three real totals visible at once and check plainly marked $0, the reader can see the nudge and overrule it in one click.

7 · The system

One table, one resolver, one guest endpoint.

Everything below is from the verified system design, and every claim in it was re-opened against the file it cites. Section references are to that document.

The link — public.invoice_links §2.1–§2.3

A side table, not columns on invoices: the invoice UPDATE paths are hash-pinned, and a side table keeps a revoke history. It stores the raw token, deliberately. Four server producers must re-emit the same link for the invoice's life or K2's "stays as a receipt" fails; hash-only would force a remint on every send. The hash's only value — "a database read yields no working link" — is void with the raw token in the adjacent column anyway. Protection is the table's posture: RLS on with zero policies, and all grants revoked from PUBLIC, anon and authenticated. Only service_role can touch it. The comparison case, 00548's board shares, hashes because its resolver is anon-callable from a browser; ours is not.

Token
encode(extensions.gen_random_bytes(32),'hex') — 256 bits, schema-qualified per the 00282 incident, with a ^[0-9a-f]{64}$ CHECK and a unique index.
Statuses
active (payable, then a receipt) · revoked (a Regenerate) · closed (set by void_invoice). One partial unique index enforces one active link per invoice.
Minting
An AFTER UPDATE OF status trigger on invoices, firing on sent, partially_paid or paid. Proven against every writer: public.issue_invoice (status write at 00318:181-190), app_private.issue_invoice_for_actor which duplicates rather than delegates (00511:4071-4080 — the ceremony path, and the reason a trigger beats editing issue_invoice), the trade-draw and executed-on-paper paths which insert draft then call issue_invoice, and apply_invoice_payment_effects, which can move a draft straight to partially_paid. All nineteen migration inserts use draft; nothing inserts an invoice already sent.
Never raises
The trigger is SECURITY DEFINER and sits on the money path, so its body ends EXCEPTION WHEN OTHERS THEN RETURN NEW. A missing link is recoverable through ensure_invoice_link; a failed payment settlement is not.
Backfill
Every sent | partially_paid | paid invoice gets a token in the migration. Draft and void are skipped.

The read path §3

resolve_invoice_link(p_token, p_record_view default true) returns one scrubbed jsonb and is granted to authenticated and service_role — not anon, mirroring resolve_plan_transmittal (00429:1908-1911). It is called only server-side, from a server-only module with the service client. It is VOLATILE, because it writes view_count.

The payload carries the invoice's numbers and dates, its line items, its payments, the studio's letterhead, the designer's and client's display names, payment_options and pay.rails / pay.processing. It carries no uuid at any depth. The forbidden list is explicit: every *_id, internal_notes, any email or phone, every Stripe identifier, void_reason, voided_at, the A/R and reminder columns, the token itself, the return nonce, and each payment's reference, recorded_by and note. It is enforced twice — a SQL assertion that walks the payload's keys, and a recursive TypeScript parser that rejects a forbidden key at any depth before the page ever renders.

The letterhead comes from resolve_studio_identity(p_project_id => …, p_designer_id => …, p_studio_id => …) (00571:1318), all three named. That is the two-studio fix: a designer who works under two studios must never send a bill under the wrong name.

Dead-link semantics, and the one exception

A malformed token, an unknown token, a revoked link, a draft invoice and a void invoice with nothing in flight all return the same NULL, and the page renders one dead sheet. No timing branch, no oracle, no difference a prober can measure.

The exception is the settling sheet. A closed link whose invoice carries a pending or requires_refund payment resolves to a minimal payload — letterhead, invoice number, one sentence, studio contact; no amounts, no chooser, no act. Without it, a guest charged real money seconds before the studio voided the invoice is told "This link is no longer good," with no receipt and no address to write to. void_invoice refusing while an attempt is processing closes the common half of that window; the settling sheet closes the rest.

The guest rail §4

A new edge function, invoice-link-checkout, with verify_jwt = false — the tenth such function in the repo, alongside stripe-webhook, fulfillment-evidence and site-request-guest. It takes { token, method } where method is card, us_bank_account or check. create-checkout-session is not flipped public: it serves PO payments and direct orders for signed-in callers and for iOS, and its every branch stays byte-identical.

Origin-gated
No wildcard CORS. The function accepts a request whose Origin is absent — the Worker's own server-side call — or equal to CLIENT_PORTAL_URL. Anything else is 403. iOS opens the page, not the function.
Uniform errors
Malformed, unknown, revoked, closed, draft, void or zero-balance all collapse to 404 invoice_not_found. A constant response floor equalises the timing difference between a valid token's round trip and an invalid one's regex test.
The link payer
The payer is coalesce(invoices.client_id, projects.client_id) where one exists. Where none does — and that is the core population, not an edge — the link itself is the identity. A rostered household that never signed up has no profiles row at all: 00018:2-5 dropped designer_clients.client_id NOT NULL, and profiles.id is a primary key referencing auth.users. The rejected alternative was a synthetic payer, which means minting a real Supabase auth account for a homeowner who never asked for one, silently, at the moment they click Pay.
First-class identity
invoice_link_id becomes a first-class identity term everywhere payer_id is one — the attempt table under an exclusive-or CHECK, the Stripe metadata on both Session and PaymentIntent, assertInvoiceSessionIdentity, the webhook's own invoice-checkout-integrity.ts, and finalize_invoice_checkout_attempt / recover_invoice_checkout_session_evidence, both of which gain p_invoice_link_id. Not a nullable column only the claim function knows about.
Actor change
A stranger who opens a link Checkout and walks away must not lock the household out of paying its own invoice. The payer/customer check moves below the supersede branch and 'actor_changed' joins the supersede reasons: fail the stale pending payment, mark the attempt superseded, return the session id so the caller can expire it. Belt-and-braces, an hourly pg_cron sweep flips claimed and session_created attempts older than 24 hours to expired, with a job_runs row. processing is never swept — that is ACH money in flight.
The return nonce
A Stripe Session's success_url is visible in the dashboard, in event payloads, in webhook logs and in any export, indefinitely. The token is a permanent bearer credential, so it does not go there. Each attempt carries a 32-byte return_nonce; Stripe returns to /pay/return/<nonce>, a route that resolves the nonce server-side and 303s to /pay/<token> with the same query. Applied to both rails, so Stripe never sees a token at all.
The receipt reaches someone
In the exact payer-less case this feature exists for, today's resolveRecipient resolves nothing and short-circuits: no receipt, no failure letter, no inbox row. So the webhook persists the email Checkout collects onto invoice_links.payer_email and addresses the letter there. The folio then tells the designer the truth: "the receipt goes to the address they give at checkout."

The letters, and the surfaces that point at the page §5–§8

A new _shared/invoice-links.ts gives the functions invoiceLinkUrl, invoiceReturnUrl and ensureInvoiceLinkUrl. Four letters change one line each: the receipt and the ACH-failure notice in the webhook, the send letter, and the reminder. Each becomes await ensureInvoiceLinkUrl(...) ?? <today's literal> — a null return is the safety valve, so a letter never ships a broken address, it just falls back to today's behaviour.

Two consequences are stated rather than discovered later. A comment sitting directly above the line being edited records the opposite ruling — "/invoices/<id> stays: the Patina iOS app claims /invoices/* in its applinks entitlement" — so after this change a signed-in iOS client's receipt link opens Safari, not the app. That is a legitimate K1 consequence; the comment is rewritten in the same commit and the reversal is recorded in the DECISIONS entry. And the in-app inbox's deep_link stays /invoices/<id>, because it routes the iOS inbox by id — so the emailed link and the inbox link for the same event now land on different surfaces. Harmless, and said out loud so nobody "fixes" it.

The signed-in surfaces §6, §7, §8

The letterbox keeps its ledger line — number, balance, due — and gains one act, Open the invoice. Settle-in-place and the separate print sheet are retired, but not in the same deploy: the page ships additively first, both surfaces live, and the retirement is a later, separately revertible step after the functions have soaked. The folio gains Copy link for every non-draft, non-void invoice, and Regenerate — which refuses while a payment is in progress, because the Stripe return address is bound to the old attempt. The print sheet folds into the token page as a @media print block, which is also where the print-only colophon line lives. iOS keeps its native invoice screen; its Pay button swaps create-checkout-session for get_invoice_link and opens /pay/<token> in the SafariView it already presents. The legacy path stays valid until that build ships.

9 · What the review caught

Forty-nine findings, seven of them blockers.

A separate reviewer, in a separate context, attacked the design and the architecture against the code they cite, with no severity filter. It returned 49 findings — 7 blockers, 17 major, 21 minor, 4 nits — and one sentence that shaped the rest of the program: "do not ship W1 as written." All seven blockers are fixed in the architecture this deck presents.

#The findingWhy it matteredThe ruling
M1 create-checkout-session throws on a falsy payer_id at :853, and the reconcile path repeats it at :973. A link claim echoes payer_id NULL, so every guest checkout would throw before reaching Stripe. The feature would not have worked once. Widen payerId to string | null, add invoiceLinkId, and replace both truthiness guards with "exactly one of payer_id / invoice_link_id is present" — matching the database CHECK.
M2 The webhook carries its own identity assertion in invoice-checkout-integrity.ts, independent of the shared one, and the design treated the webhook as a letters-only edit. On a link attempt all three sides are NULL, so both terms evaluate false and drop out silently — no error, just a weaker check. The guest rail would settle on customer id, invoice id and attempt id alone, and nobody would notice until an incident. invoice_link_id joins the select at :207, the two claim types, and the mismatch expression. A tampered-link-id case goes in beside the existing tampered-payer one.
M3 claim_invoice_checkout_attempt raises on an actor change at 00428:294-296, above the supersede branch — and nothing in 573 migrations sweeps a stale attempt. Today it is unreachable, because only the household can claim. The link rail makes it a lockout: one stranger who opens a link Checkout and walks away blocks the household from paying its own invoice, permanently. The design had sold this as a safety property. Move the check below the supersede branch and add 'actor_changed' as a supersede reason. Add an hourly pg_cron sweep for abandoned attempts older than 24 hours, never touching processing. S1 A fourth allowlist: next-pwa's NetworkFirst catch-all, with a 30-day cache. Cache Storage does not honour no-store, so the middleware header would not have covered it. On a non-OpenNext build, every /pay/<token> response — a full invoice plus a live pay link — would be stored in the visitor's browser for a month, keyed by the token URL. A NetworkOnly entry before the catch-all for all six bearer prefixes, asserted in Playwright. Re-checking the claim found the catch-all is inert on the deployed Worker — withPWA is the identity function under OpenNext and public/sw.js is gitignored — so this deck does not claim production is caching pay links today. G1 moneyInWords does not spell money in words, and rounds cents away. Three directions built their standing sentence on it. A $9,125.49 balance would have read "$9,125" beside a Pay button charging $9,130.49. Banned from this page. Every figure is formatCurrency, including inside accessible names.
G2 The $18,250 invoice total was invented; the fixture pins only the balance. The design's own "$1,520 gap" was an artefact of the fabrication, and both proposed remedies preserved it. Total $16,730.00, received $7,605.00, balance $9,125.00. Every visible figure now derives from a fixture amount or arithmetic on one.
G5 The design chose "send a null card rate and hold the act." The code had already ruled the other way. studio_billing_settings rows are written only by a studio admin — no backfill, no trigger — so most studios have no row, and the held state would have disabled card payment for almost every invoice Patina sends. Three things the architecture had to adapt

Not every ruling survived contact with the code. Three are worth recording, because each one would have been a silent defect.

  • designer_clients has no project key. The ruling said the receipt fallback should key on designer plus the invoice's project. That column does not exist. The implementable form is: invoice_links.payer_email first, then designer plus client_id IS NULL through maybeSingle() — which resolves only when exactly one email-only roster row exists, matching the unique index, and returns null otherwise. This is the one ruling not applicable verbatim, and it is reported as such.
  • The PWA cache does not reach the deployed Worker. See S1 above. The fix ships; the claim is corrected.
  • void_invoice's body is the renamed legacy function. The edits go into _void_invoice_authorized_legacy_00397, not into public.void_invoice — which is now a thin authority wrapper created at 00397:1454-1477. Grafting from the wrong one would silently drop the authorization wrapper.

12 · Rulings owed Kody

Four, each with a recommendation.

Everything else the program raised has been ruled — including the seven the architect left open and the six the design lead left open. These four need Kody.

1 · Namespace id
The PAY_LINK_RATELIMIT namespace id. Cloudflare rate-limit namespaces are account-scoped integers chosen by the author, and no Patina Worker has one today. Recommendation: pick any unused integer and record it, so no future Worker reuses it. It must be settled before W2 ships, and the fail-loud behaviour above is what catches it if it isn't.
2 · Void
"Withdrawn by {studio}" versus the bare dead link. K2 says dead, and the dead sheet deliberately carries no letterhead, no studio name and no amount, so void, revoked and never-existed are indistinguishable. The softer state genuinely leaks nothing — the token is unguessable, so naming the studio to its holder tells them what they already knew. Recommendation: keep dead for now. The settling sheet (Plate 6) now exists, so the softer state is a two-line variation on a sheet Kody can look at rather than an abstract choice.
3 · Stripe receipts
Are Stripe's own receipt emails switched on in live mode? They are dashboard-gated and off by default in test mode. Recommendation: turn them on. Patina's own receipt is the primary letter and now reaches the Checkout-collected email, but a payment page's first guest payment is the wrong moment to discover that neither party sent anything.
4 · The Stripe key
The standing STRIPE_SECRET_KEY account mismatch (docs/ops/stripe-rail-verification.md). Two different Stripe accounts are in play and the key almost certainly still holds the sandbox account's. Recommendation: fix it before W1 ships, not before W4. This is a precondition on the whole feature — every other guard in this deck assumes the money goes somewhere real.

Decisions recorded, not asked

Three were ruled during the program and are noted here so nobody reopens them by accident. The host is client.patina.cloud/pay/<token> — one Worker, one AASA, no new DNS or environment split, and pay.patina.cloud is ruled never, because K2 makes these URLs permanent. Links do not expire, for the same reason. And view_count is kept for support diagnostics and not surfaced — a homeowner should not feel watched, and putting "opened 3 times, last Tuesday" in front of the studio is exactly the edge Vision §6 guards.
