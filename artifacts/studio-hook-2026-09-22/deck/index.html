Diagnosis

The ask, in Kody’s words

“They come into the designer portal, get excited to set things up, then pop back out into their manual ways to drive jobs. Studios need to feel like they are saving time by coming to Patina, not having to learn a whole new system.” The problem statement, as given

What hooked is allowed to mean here is already settled. VISION §4: you won’t notice Patina. Success is time saved and fewer surprises. Never engagement — no dashboards, no streaks, no returning-user target on the studio surface.

So this deck measures a removed task, not a returning user. And it says the substitution out loud: this is not a hook program. It is the program that finds out whether a hook is possible for the one studio we have.

The ask has two halves. Saving time we can measure in minutes on one real bill. Not learning a whole new system we measure by counting what she had to be told.

03 §5–§6 · 08 G9
Diagnosis

The outside view: what held up

Mechanism

Switching cost and incumbent habit, not misunderstanding. Kim & Kankanhalli (2009) found switching costs the central mediator of resistance to a new system. Polites & Karahanna (2012) found inertia — habit plus sunk cost — fully mediates it: people do not weigh the new tool and prefer the old one; they never really weigh it.

The commercial literature converges independently on “quiet reversion”: staff keep using spreadsheets beside the new system when it adds re-entry without an immediate personal payoff.

Patterns that move switchers

  • Never start from zero — her own data in the product fast. Notion’s own principle: the quicker users see their own data, the faster they understand the value.
  • A human 1:1 first session. Superhuman made a 30–60 minute call mandatory for years, then graduated out of it on its own schedule.
  • Progressive disclosure over setup wizards (NN/g): surface help beside each step instead of front-loading it.
  • Checklists rarely finish, and guidance converges on three-to-five items; past five, completion is reported to fall. Patina’s has six.
  • Streaks and badges backfire on weekly-cadence tools, and are read as a symptom that activation was never solved.

Incumbents

  • Paid migration — Studio Designer’s desktop-to-cloud move is a $4,000 line item — still draws “steep learning curve” reviews.
  • DesignFiles ships phase templates so a studio can “skip the setup”; Programa’s web clipper turns sourcing itself into data entry.
  • HoneyBook ingests the studio’s existing brochures, proposals, questionnaires and old contracts and builds the templates from them.
  • QuickBooks offers assisted migration — free, capacity-gated, capped at one guided session.
  • Easy-in, hard-out erodes trust even in a tool people otherwise love.

Diagnosis

Why she pops out

She doesn’t pop out because she failed to learn Patina. She pops out because her live jobs are still somewhere else, and Patina’s first hour asks for setup, a lead-to-discovery ceremony, and a curriculum while giving back nothing she can hand to a client or a vendor.

Patina has not yet demonstrated that it removes recurring work for an independent growing studio. That is the honest starting position, and it is why the first test must cross from work outside Patina rather than repeat a successful act on the house already in it.

Still competing, and not ruled out

  • Switching effort — the re-entry cost of bringing a live job in.
  • Missing functionality — Leah’s one recorded departure went back to the old portal seeking completeness, not fleeing friction.
  • Reliability — whether the figures can be trusted without checking them twice.
  • Delegation — no willing non-founder colleague has been established.
  • Unclear commercial terms — no pricing page exists anywhere a studio can see, and the Pledge band renders to every studio unflagged, against VISION §3.

If completeness is the operative one, the finding will be “she finished the bill and still keeps the book elsewhere, because the rest of the house is not in Patina.” That is a different program.

Program

How we measure, without a dashboard

Measure a removed task

  • Normal-method active minutes, against all studio time in Patina: setup, search, entry, review, correction, support, export, duplicate maintenance.
  • First use kept separate from repeat. Setup is never amortised against imaginary future work.
  • Kody’s time shown separately from hers; machine wait separate from human effort.
  • Remembered baselines are estimates, and are labelled as estimates.
  • Never recreate collectible work for a stopwatch.
  • Where the invoice went afterwards, and the minutes that took, counted as duplicate work in the net.

The ‘told’ count

Every thing Kody had to say for her to proceed, with the surface it concerned. The honest readout sentence is: she had to be told N things to finish one bill. That is the only observable form of “learning a whole new system.”

Continuation rule

Two natural comparable opportunities with positive net savings; the later one without founder preparation; no financial or privacy error; and a voluntary repeat choice. Anything less is not continuation.

One studio establishes a case, not retention causality. No opportunity is not churn. Project creation is not saved work. Nothing here becomes a studio-facing tile, target or streak.

Ask at the end: “What did this remove from your day?”
