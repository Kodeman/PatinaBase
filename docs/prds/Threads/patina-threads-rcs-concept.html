01 · The Problem

The portal is where projects
go quiet.

Anyone who has run a job site knows the pattern. The receiver at the warehouse dock doesn't log into a web app. The installer standing in a client's driveway isn't going to remember a password. And the homeowner — the person we built all of this for — gets a "please log in to review your selections" email on a Tuesday and opens it Saturday, if at all.

Every login wall we put between a person and a five-second decision converts momentum into lag. The industry research in our own knowledge base says it plainly: designers already lose 60–70% of their time to administration, and the single most communication-hungry phase of a project — installation — demands daily contact. That contact happens today over untracked personal texts, voicemail, and hallway promises. The portal has the record; the text thread has the truth.

The core insight

Patina doesn't have an information problem. It has a last-fifty-feet problem: the distance between our beautifully structured project data and the thumb of the person who needs to answer one question about it.

98%

60–70%

The homeowner's friction

Approvals stall for days because "review in the app" is a chore, not a moment. Deposits sit unpaid because the checkout is three screens away from where they read about it.

The trade's friction

Delivery crews and installers won't adopt our software — ever. Their coordination happens by text or it doesn't happen, and today none of it lands in the project record.

The designer's friction

The designer becomes the human router — relaying the client's "yes," the freight company's window, the installer's punch item. Skill time spent on switchboard work.

02 · The Opening

Meet people where they
already are.

RCS is the upgrade text messaging waited two decades for — and it recently crossed the line from promising to production-ready. Twilio's RCS Business Messaging is generally available, reaches Android and iPhone (iOS 18.1+) natively, covers every major U.S. carrier, and — this is the part that makes it viable for us — falls back to SMS/MMS automatically when a device or connection can't do RCS. One API, one send, every phone.

For a brand whose entire thesis is trust and provenance, the killer feature isn't the carousels. It's verification. Every Patina message arrives with our name, our mark, and a verified badge — in an era when consumers rightly assume an unknown number texting about a delivery is a scam. Trust is the product; RCS lets the channel carry it.

Why now

Apple's RCS adoption removed the last coverage barrier, and Twilio manages carrier registration, device capability checks, and fallback for us — so a solo-founder-sized team can ship a Fortune-500-grade messaging experience without owning any of the plumbing.

03 · Design Principles

Rules the feature lives by.

i

The thread is the portal.

For clients and trades, Threads isn't a notification system pointing at our software — it is the software. Every routine decision must be completable inside the conversation. If a flow requires a login to finish, we've failed at that flow. (The full portal remains, for those who want depth.)

ii

One question, one thumb, five seconds.

We already design Leah's review surface around a five-second budget. Threads extends that dignity to everyone. Each message asks exactly one thing and offers tap-sized answers. If a decision needs paragraphs, it doesn't belong in a text — it gets scheduled as a conversation with the designer.

iii

Fallback is a first-class design, not a degradation.

Every rich pattern is designed twice, deliberately: the RCS form and its SMS twin. The plain-text version has its own grammar — numbered replies, one short branded link, 160-character discipline. A client on a flip phone in Door County gets an experience we designed, not one we shrugged into.

iv

The designer stays in the studio.

Threads talks to clients and trades so the designer doesn't have to relay. Everything a thread learns — an approval, a delivery window, a punch-list photo — lands as structured data in the Designer Portal and Mission Control. Designers never manage a second inbox, and nothing about this adds a single second to Leah's day.

v

Quiet by default, human on demand.

Threads speaks only when the project needs something, inside quiet hours, in Patina's voice — warm, unpretentious, brief. And every automated exchange carries a visible trapdoor to a person. The moment a reply doesn't fit the expected shape, a human gets the baton; the client is never trapped arguing with a robot.

07 · Journey Deep-Dive · Client

The selection approval.

Problem

Approvals are the project's heartbeat and its slowest organ. A client who'd say "yes" in five seconds face-to-face takes four days by portal email — and every idle day pushes lead times, deliveries, and the designer's schedule downstream.

Function

When the designer marks FF&E items client-ready in the Designer Portal, Threads composes a Shelf — one card per piece, imagery from the catalog, Leah's note as the description. Each card carries Approve, Ask a question, and See it in your room (deep link into the AR app for those who have it). Approvals write directly to the FF&E schedule; questions open a relay to the designer's Portal inbox with the item attached.

Placement

Design & Selections phase. Triggered by designer action, throttled to one batch per day, sent inside quiet hours. A read-but-silent card earns exactly one gentle nudge after 48 hours — read receipts mean we never nag someone who hasn't seen it yet.

Vision

Approval cycles collapse from days to hours. The FF&E schedule becomes self-updating. And the client's memory of the project is a warm conversation, not a stack of login prompts.

UI detail worth noticing

The user's tap lands as a visible reply bubble — "Approve: Halvorsen Credenza" — so the thread reads as a record both parties can scroll. Confirmation follows with the running tally ("2 of 3 approved"), giving the same progress-through-material feel as the app's Patina Path.

08 · Journey Deep-Dive · Client

The delivery window.

Problem

Delivery scheduling is a three-way phone-tag between freight, client, and designer — with the designer as unpaid dispatcher. Missed windows mean redelivery fees, warehouse storage charges, and the worst client experience in the whole journey.

Function

When a shipment reaches the local hub, Threads offers the client the carrier's available windows as chips. The selection writes to the project schedule, notifies the receiver's thread, and sets two automatic confirmations: day-before and morning-of, the second carrying live context ("crew of two, they'll call from the driveway"). A reschedule chip is always present until the cutoff.

Placement

Procurement & Delivery phase, triggered by carrier milestone events (or, in early builds, by ops marking "at hub" in Mission Control). Windows only offered inside the client's stated availability preferences captured at kickoff.

Vision

White-glove logistics feeling without white-glove headcount: the client picks a window in one thumb-press, and nobody at Patina touched the exchange unless something went sideways.

UI detail worth noticing

The morning-of card includes an Add to calendar action and the delivery crew's first names. Small, human, and it cuts no-shows more than any reminder cadence — people don't stand up "Marcus and Dee" the way they stand up a tracking number.

09 · Journey Deep-Dive · Trade

Install day, on the record.

Problem

Anyone who's coordinated an install knows: the crew has the address in one text, the gate code in another, the "client's dog is friendly but loud" note nowhere. Issues get photographed to a personal number and die there. The project record ends where the field work begins.

Function

The evening before, each crew contact receives the Site Card: address (tappable to maps), parking and access notes, contact, run-of-show. Morning-of, a one-tap On our way chip timestamps departure and quietly notifies the client's thread. During work, texting a photo files it to the project with the sender and time attached; adding a word like "damage" or "short" flags it as a punch item and routes it to the ops Approval Inbox. A Wrapped up chip closes the visit and triggers the client walkthrough prompt.

Placement

Install & Close phase. SMS-first by design — a two-year-old flip phone can run this entire flow. RCS devices simply get the richer card and map action for free.

Vision

Field truth flows into the same record as design intent. Punch lists build themselves from the driveway. And trades experience Patina as the easiest client they work with — which, in a three-sided market, is recruiting.

UI detail worth noticing

Trades never get marketing voice. Their grammar is terse, all signal: address, time, codes, confirm. Respecting the difference between a homeowner's thread and a crew's thread is the design.

10 · Journey Deep-Dive · Money

The payment moment.

Problem

The gap between "the client said yes" and "the deposit cleared" is where projects stall and lead times slip. Emailed invoices get lost; portal checkouts get postponed to "tonight," which means Thursday.

Function

When a proposal or order total is approved, Threads sends a summary card — pieces, total, what happens next — with Review & pay opening a Stripe-hosted checkout inside an in-thread webview (the Doorway). Payment confirmation posts back to the thread with a receipt card. On the SMS twin, the same Stripe link travels as a short branded URL.

Placement

Rail A only — Designer Selections, where Patina is merchant of record. Fires once per invoice, plus one read-aware reminder before the quote's price-hold expires.

Vision

Time-to-deposit measured in minutes. The most anxious moment of the client journey handled with the same calm as everything else — and handled where they already are.

The hard rule

No payment data ever lives in the thread. The thread carries intent; Stripe carries the card. Nobody is ever asked to text a number, and the ledger — not Stripe, and certainly not Twilio — remains the financial source of truth. Copy near money never uses urgency mechanics; the reminder mentions the price-hold date once, plainly, and lets it be.

11 · The Fallback Grammar

Every rich pattern has
a plain-text twin.

Twilio detects device capability per message and falls back automatically — but automatic only handles delivery, not dignity. We author both forms of every template so the SMS version reads as intentional. These are the standing rules:

RCS patternSMS twinRules of the grammar
The Card richOne message: context line + short branded link for imagery + explicit reply instruction.Lead with who we are ("Patina:") since no branding survives. One idea per message. Link goes to a mobile page that needs no login for viewing.
The Shelf (carousel)Numbered list, one line per item: name + price. Reply codes: number = approve, Q+number = question.Five items maximum, same as RCS. Codes are stable for the life of the decision — replying "2" on Thursday still works.
The ChipsNumbered options in the message body: "Reply 1, 2, or 3."Numbers over words — thumbs and autocorrect are the enemy. YES/NO accepted as aliases where natural. Every set reserves a number for "none of these / talk to a person."
The Doorway (webview)Short branded link (patina.cloud/…) opening the same Stripe or signature page in the browser.Always our domain, never a raw Stripe or bit.ly URL — the link is the trust signal in SMS. One link per message, ever.
Rich media / photosMMS image where supported; link where not.Inbound photos work identically on both — MMS capture is the great equalizer for field work.
Read-aware nudgesNot available — SMS has no read receipts.Fallback contacts get time-based cadence instead (one reminder, 72h), tuned gentler because we can't know if they've seen it.
Character discipline

SMS twins are written to land in one or two segments: 160 characters is a design constraint, treated like column width in good typography. If a message wants to be three segments, it wants to be a link.

12 · Conversation Intelligence

Structured first, smart second,
human always.

Threads is a state machine before it is a chatbot. Every outbound message declares what shapes of reply it expects; most inbound traffic is a tap or a reply code that resolves deterministically. The interesting design is what happens when a reply doesn't fit.

Tier 1

Deterministic. Chip payloads, reply codes, YES/NO, STOP/HELP. Resolved instantly, written to the project record. Expected to carry ~80% of all traffic.

Tier 2

Interpreted. Free-text replies pass through intent classification in the ML sidecar — reschedule, question-about-item, confusion, frustration, off-topic. Confident matches get resolved or gracefully redirected ("Sounds like Saturday's out — here are fresh windows"). The thread may clarify once. It never argues.

Tier 3

Human. Low confidence, any frustration signal, any request for a person, anything touching money disputes or design opinion → the thread says so honestly ("Handing this to a person — you'll hear back today") and escalates. No dead ends, no loops.

Decisions already implied by our doctrine

Edge

Twilio webhooks terminate at a Supabase Edge Function, not the homelab — inbound texts must survive a Proxmox reboot. This is exactly the "third-party webhook receiver" carve-out in our standing architecture rule.

Monolith

@strata/threads is a package in the monorepo, not a service. State machine, template registry, and send scheduler live with the rest of the brain; no new deployment surface beyond the edge function.

Truth

Twilio delivery logs are reconciled against our own thread event table — same posture as Stripe vs. the ledger. The channel is never the record.

Identity

One number, one verified Patina agent, for the life of the relationship. Thread context keys off phone + project; a client with two projects gets clearly-labeled context switches, not two numbers.

Agents

Threads emits events the Agent OS already understands: Concierge Ops Copilot drafts escalation replies; Morning Brief reports overnight thread activity; the Run Log records every automated send for auditability.

14 · Consent & Conduct

Permission is the foundation.

Opt-in

Express written consent captured at project kickoff — a checkbox with plain language in the proposal/onboarding flow, never buried in terms. First message confirms it and states frequency, HELP, and STOP. Consent events (grant, revoke, scope) are written to an append-only audit table. Trades consent per-engagement via their onboarding touchpoint.

Opt-out

STOP honored instantly and universally, handled at the Twilio layer and mirrored in our record. A client who stops texting keeps full service through portal and email — opting out of a channel is never opting out of care.

Registration

RCS agent verification (brand assets, use-case review) and A2P 10DLC registration for the SMS fallback both run through Twilio's onboarding. Lead time is real — this is a Phase-0 task, started before any code.

Quiet hours

Default send window 9am–7pm recipient-local, respecting the Tuesday–Friday boundaries our designer research treats as professional standard. Only day-of logistics (a crew running late) may breach the window, and only toward participants in that day's event.

15 · Guardrails

What Threads is not.

×

Not a marketing channel.

The moment a thread sells, every future message reads as selling. Threads speaks only in service of the project the person already committed to. This restraint is a brand asset — guard it.

×

Not a replacement for the designer's voice.

Threads handles logistics so the designer's messages are reserved for the work only a designer can do. If clients start experiencing Patina as a bot with a designer attached, we've inverted the thesis. Designers are the intelligence layer; the thread is plumbing.

×

Not a second inbox for Leah.

Zero net-new obligations. Leah's exposure to Threads is the same swipe queue she already has, fed slightly better. If any part of this design leaks work onto her plate, that part gets redesigned.

×

Not a place for the whole project.

Contracts, full proposals, room scans, the ledger — deep artifacts live in deep surfaces. The thread is the hallway conversation, not the filing cabinet, and it should always feel light.

×

Not a chatbot with opinions.

The thread never improvises taste, never negotiates, never argues. Its intelligence is knowing what it's for — and handing off the instant something isn't.
