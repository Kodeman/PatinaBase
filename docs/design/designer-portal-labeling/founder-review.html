What we found

The labels ask designers
to translate before they act.

“Rooms” · “Ledgers”

Categories describe a metaphor.

Library, contacts and scans sit under “Rooms.” Orders, invoices, time and messages sit under “Ledgers.”

Name the contents: Resources and Business tools.

“Your pen” · “At rest”

Responsibility is hard to read.

“Your pen” means an action needs you. “At rest” can still contain unfinished drafts and unscheduled discovery calls.

Say Your action needed and No flagged action.

“Open” · “Draw” · “Nudge”

Verbs hide the outcome.

“Open a project” creates one. “Draw an invoice” opens a composer. “Nudge” starts a message.

Use Create, Draft, View and Message precisely.

“Schedule” · “Pieces”

One task has competing names.

Schedule can refer to project dates or an FF&E item list. Pieces, lines and products overlap in the same workflow.

Separate Project timeline from Item schedule.

“Ask the Engine”

Internal concepts take the lead.

The library also refers to shelves, teaching, accuracy and matches being sharpened. The designer’s goal is harder to spot.

Lead with finding products or describing preferences.

“The Post” · “The words”

Common tools have unfamiliar names.

Designers must discover that these mean an inbox and a glossary. “Keys” adds another term to learn.

Use Inbox, Glossary and Keyboard shortcuts.

Examples observed in the authenticated portal and traced to source. These are content findings, not a usability-test result or a claim about measured conversion.

The founder decision

Choose one language system.

All three make actions explicit. They differ in how designers recognize where they are and where to go.

APlain-language workspace

Recommended foundation

Familiar nouns for destinations. Specific verbs for actions. The least interpretation for new or occasional users.

Home
Product library
Clients & contacts
Create an invoice

Tradeoff: Less distinctive navigation language. Brand warmth moves into the supporting copy.

BTask-first workspace

Organize labels around the question, “What do I need to do?” Make entry points read like useful actions.

Review my work
Find products
Manage contacts
Bill a client

Tradeoff: Longer navigation and more repeated verbs. Destination headings still need a stable noun.

CProfessional studio

Use the language of an established design practice. Keep familiar trade terms, while removing invented portal metaphors.

Studio overview
Resource library
Studio contacts
Draft an invoice

Tradeoff: Terms such as specification book or receivables may still need a short explanation.

Direction A / Plain-language workspace

Familiar places.
Predictable actions.

Use the vocabulary designers already expect from business software.

  • Navigation names the contents: Product library, Invoices & payments.
  • Buttons state the effect: Create a project, Save time entry.
  • Statuses name the person or condition: Waiting on client, Draft.
  • Supporting text remains warm, direct and specific.

Best fit: Reduce onboarding effort across owners, designers, assistants and occasional collaborators.

Direction B / Task-first workspace

Put the next action
into the label.

Make the portal read like a set of things a designer can get done.

  • Entry points begin with a verb: Find products, Track orders.
  • Actions name their object: Add items to this project.
  • Headings orient the designer after the click: Products, Orders.
  • Statuses remain factual. “Waiting on client” must not become “Approved.”

Best fit: Designers who know their immediate goal but struggle to find the tool that does it.

Direction C / Professional studio

Sound like a design practice.
Keep the meaning visible.

Retain professional specificity without requiring designers to learn a fictional office.

  • Use familiar studio destinations: Studio overview, Project directory.
  • Keep accurate trade terms: Drawing sets, Purchase orders.
  • Expand abbreviations the first time they matter.
  • Reserve expressive language for supporting guidance, never a critical transaction.

Best fit: Experienced design studios that value industry vocabulary and a more distinctive professional voice.

The verb must match the action’s actual boundary. A review screen can say “Review authorization request.” A button that submits it must say “Send for purchasing authorization.” Do not label a draft or review step “Order now.”

Rules shared by all three directions

Clearer wording must stay truthful.

Keep these separateWhat the copy must communicate
Design agreement / ProposalA design services agreement and a product/design proposal are different documents. Do not rename both “Proposal.”
Design approval / Purchase authorization / OrderLiking a design does not authorize a purchase. An authorization does not prove a purchase order was sent.
Draft / Review / SendOpening a composer, reviewing a draft and sending it are separate actions. Name the step that actually happens.
Invoice / Payment / Payment recordCreating an invoice is not charging a card. Recording an external payment is not collecting one.
Project timeline / Item scheduleOne tracks dates, phases and milestones; the other lists specified items. Never leave both as “Schedule.”
No flagged action / CompletedA quiet project can still contain work. “At rest” should not become “Done” or “On track.”
Client balance / Vendor balanceUse direction and party: “Client balance due” versus “Vendor balance due.” “Not drawn” in the vendor-payment ladder is not unbilled client revenue.

These are wording constraints based on observed/source-defined behavior, not changes to financial or approval policies.

Recommendation

Choose A as the foundation.
Keep expertise where it helps.

Use familiar navigation and explicit actions. Keep accurate professional terms inside specialist work, with a short explanation when needed.

First: finding the work

Align navigation, page titles, search results, return links and help headings. Start with Home, Product library, Clients & contacts, Inbox and Invoices & payments.

Next: taking action

Replace ambiguous creation verbs, document metaphors and responsibility labels. Pair every disabled action or empty state with an understandable next step.

Then: completing the vocabulary

Apply the same terms to dialogs, statuses, mobile labels, tooltips, screen-reader names, notifications and help copy.

Validate with designers before rollout. Ask them to create a project, find an item, locate a client invoice, review a weekly update and identify who must act next. Compare completion and wrong turns with today’s wording. This review does not establish that any direction has already improved those measures.

Implementation starts only after a direction is approved. Label changes can preserve the existing routes and workflows. If a control is still hard to find with clear wording, record that as a separate navigation issue.
