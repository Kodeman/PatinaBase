Act I · Where we are02

What we shipped, said plainly.

Yesterday's ship made the agreement-first flow real, and it works: an executed design-services agreement unlocks the project. Everything after the unlock is what this deck is about.

Working, and staying
  • The agreement unlocks the project. Signature, countersignature, and the project opens for work. Nothing here touches it.
  • The schedule is real. Every furnishing line already carries a room, a client price, a trade cost, a document code, an expected date, and an eight-state logistics stamp.
  • The configuration sidecar is real. Finish, fabric, dimension, and option snapshots ride alongside each line.
  • Checkpoints and fingerprints work — and are keyed to the project, not to any intermediate document.
Redundant by construction
  • "Draft a furnishing proposal" mints an empty draft. Nothing carries over. The line count starts at zero.
  • The Drafting Room asks for it all again — eight facets, re-authored by hand, from a schedule that is already sitting one screen away.
  • Execution inserts twin rows. A second copy of each line lands on the schedule beside the original.
  • The chain never reads the schedule. Not once, in either direction.
The condition of production

Strata holds zero authorizations and zero executed agreements today. There is no data to migrate, no client mid-signature, no purchase order pointing at a twin. Whatever we rule, the pivot is close to free — and it will not be free again in six months.

Act I · The wounds04

Three wounds, and they compound.

Retyping is tedious. The three below are not tedious — they stop the work, and two of them are visible to the client.

One · purchase-order death

On a commercial project, a schedule line with no authorization behind it cannot be purchased. The purchase-authority guard is right to exist. But because the chain never links back to the schedule, every original line stays unauthorized forever — only the twins carry provenance, and the twins have no room, no code, and no dates.

Net effect: the schedule you specified from is the one you cannot buy from.

Two · the client blackout

Selections, the delivery pipeline, and the budget all read the schedule directly. On a commercial project those reads are closed, and nothing was opened in their place. The client's three most-visited panels render empty on exactly the projects with the most money in them.

The client sees an engaged project with nothing in it. So do we, when we open their view.

Three · the fourth restatement

The working budget is typed by hand — a fourth statement of numbers the schedule already carries, kept true only by discipline. Every checkpoint published from it is a photograph of a number nobody derived.

A studio that restates a total four times will eventually be wrong in three places.

None of these is a bug in the ship. They are all the same design decision, seen from three sides: the money document was built beside the record instead of on top of it.

Act II · The idea05

The schedule is the proposal.

Sign the record. Don't retype it.

A furnishings authorization stops being a document you author and becomes a selection you make on the schedule. You tick the lines that are ready, name a deposit, and release them for authorization. The client signs the lines. The lines remember that they were signed — the same lines, in the same rooms, with the same codes and dates and configuration they had that morning.

The designer's act

Release for authorization. A head act on the schedule. Not a new document to draft; a set of lines to choose.

The instrument

Authorization № 3. A numbered, immutable record of what was signed, at what price, with what deposit — a receipt, not a workspace.

The schedule's memory

Authorized, in place. The line keeps its signed price, gains a second stamp, and its purchase-order cell goes live when the deposit clears.

What follows from one sentence

The twin rows stop existing. The room, the code, the dates, and the configuration are never dropped because they are never copied. The client's Selections panel has something to read again — because what it reads is what the designer specified. And the budget can be derived, because the numbers all live in one place.

The anatomy, top to bottom
  • Authority band — what is signed and what it permits. The agreement lives here, and so does the count of live authorizations.
  • Budget panel — the derived grid and the checkpoint history.
  • Schedule — the rooms, the lines, both stamp tracks, the release act.
  • Coordination — parties, site requests, trade scopes in progress.
  • Care — after install: warranty, service, the piece's life.
And in the Studio Drawer
  • Hours — time against the engagement, invoiced on the agreement's terms. Never on a furnishings instrument.
  • Orders — purchase orders, vendor confirmations, freight. Fed by authorized lines; visible only to the studio.
  • Trade costs and bids — the numbers the studio negotiates. They inform the client price. They never appear beside it.

The drawer is where the studio's half of the commerce lives. Nothing in it has a client-facing counterpart, which is why it can stay plain.

Act II · Principles07

Six rules the rest of the deck obeys.

Every screen that follows can be checked against these. Where a screen breaks one, it says so on the glass.

One

Author once.

A fact is typed where it is first known and never again. The room is chosen when the line is created; nothing downstream asks for it a second time.

Two

Sign the record.

An instrument points at lines. It does not contain copies of them. What was signed and what is being bought are the same rows.

Three

Link, never twin.

Execution writes provenance onto existing lines and advances their state. It inserts nothing. The schedule's line count does not change when a client signs.

Four

Derive, never restate.

Scheduled and Authorized are computed from the lines. Target is the one number a human authors — because it is the only one that is an intention rather than a fact.

Five

Instruments are immutable.

Once sent, an authorization cannot be edited. The recovery act is always the same two words: void and supersede. A voided instrument frees its lines.

Six

Quiet money.

The client price is the only number that crosses. Trade cost, markup, bids, and holdbacks stay in the Studio Drawer, on every screen, without exception.

Act IV · Trade work14

Trade work is not a furnishing with a person attached.

Drapery fabrication, millwork install, tile setting, paint — these are scopes of work. They are bid, engaged, performed, inspected, and accepted. They get their own instrument: Trade scope · № 1.

Draft scope Bids Select Release for authorization Client signs Engaged In progress Substantially complete Accepted Final payment
Phase 1 · the spine

Draft a scope, record the bids you already have, select one, release for authorization, engage the sub, mark complete, accept. Everything on the screens that follow.

The smallest thing that makes trade work purchasable and client-visible.

Phase 2 · sent requests

Requests for quote sent out of the portal on the coordination rails the project already uses, with the responses landing back on the bid ledger.

Candidate, not decision — ruling R3.

Phase 3 · progress billing

Draws against percentage complete, retainage held to acceptance, and a final release. Real construction money, and a real amount of work.

Candidate, not decision — ruling R4.

What it shares with a furnishings authorization
  • It is released, signed, and immutable. Same verb, same ceremony, same void-and-supersede recovery.
  • It appears on the schedule as a line in the room where the work happens, with its own instrument stamp.
  • It carries a client price only. The bids sit in the Studio Drawer.
  • It counts in the budget under a Trade work category, in its room.
What is different
  • A party, not a vendor. The sub or installer comes from the project's parties, not the product library.
  • A bid ledger. Competing numbers per party, with one selected. Nothing like it exists for goods.
  • Engagement is a second act. The client authorizes; the studio then engages the sub. Two events, two dates.
  • It ends in acceptance, not installation — and acceptance is a ceremony with a signatory. That is ruling R2.

R2 · who accepts

The client is the one paying and the one living with it. The designer is the one who can tell whether a return has been mitred properly. Both is the honest answer and the slowest one.

Why acceptance, not "installed"

Goods arrive; work is judged. A trade journey that ends at "installed" has no room for a punch list, and a punch list with no state to sit in becomes an email thread.

What acceptance releases

The balance invoice, the line's move to complete on the schedule, and the client's journey stepper reaching its last step. One act, three consequences, all visible.

Split prices, one instrument  A scope covering two rooms appears as two schedule lines carrying the same instrument stamp and summing to the signed total. The rooms stay the organising fact of the project; the instrument stays the organising fact of the money.

Act VI · Underneath20

What changes underneath, honestly.

The only slide in this deck that names code. Eight changes, none of them large, and one deletion that pays for most of them.

Additive
  • A nullable source_ffe_item_id on the authorization line table, mutually exclusive with the existing proposal source. One of the two is set, never both.
  • Fingerprint keys gain the schedule line's id, so compose-then-sign drift is detected against the real row rather than a copy of it.
  • Instrument totals are computed — the sum of the selected lines' totals, plus an explicit deposit percent stored on the instrument.
  • Room names are persisted on the snapshot so a signed document still reads correctly if a room is later renamed.
Behavioural
  • Execution links instead of inserting. A provenance update and a status ratchet on rows that already exist. No new rows, ever.
  • A guard against double authorization — one schedule line may sit in at most one live instrument. Voiding frees it.
  • Client reads move to narrow client-safe views. The row-level closure stays closed; the views expose the client price, the room, the journey, and nothing else.
  • The empty-draft path retires. create_furnishing_wave_draft and the Drafting-Room re-entry route both go, along with the eight facets they served.
On the old word

The shipped tables are named furnishing_waves and furnishing_wave_items. They are the last place the old word survives, and they become furnishing_authorization_* in the same pass. After that, nothing anyone reads — designer, client, or maintainer — uses a word the studio never said out loud.

The cost of doing it now

Zero authorizations and zero executed agreements exist in production. No backfill, no dual-read window, no client mid-signature, no purchase order pointing at a row that is about to change meaning. This is the cheapest this change will ever be, and the price rises with the first real project.

Act VI · Edges21

The edges, and what each one does.

Ten cases that decide whether this holds up in a real studio. Each has a stated behaviour; four of them also have a ruling attached.

Price moves between compose and sign

The fingerprint catches it at the moment of signature. The client is told the document has changed and is not asked to sign a stale one. Void & supersede — never a silent re-price.

Price moves after signature

The signed price stands. The line shows the delta glyph, and the designer either drafts a delta line for the difference or absorbs it. Both are recorded.

Partial rooms

Perfectly normal. The room header reads "4 of 5" and the instrument lists exactly what was ticked. A room is a grouping, not a unit of sale.

Mixed goods and trade in one instrument

Not allowed in Phase 1. Goods are authorized on a furnishings authorization, work on a trade scope. They read differently, they are performed differently, and they end differently.

Allowance ceilings

An allowance authorizes its ceiling: "Allowance · up to 4,000". Resolving at or under the ceiling carries the authorization forward to the chosen piece with no new signature. Ruling R5 settles the wording.

Allowance overage

Resolving above the ceiling does not silently expand the authorization. The excess becomes a delta line, released in the next instrument like anything else.

A line in two live instruments

Refused by a guard, not by a warning. The selection surface shows the line as ineligible with the reason "in authorization № 2", and the database will not accept it either way.

Removing a line after sending

There is no such act. A sent instrument is immutable; the recovery is void and supersede, which frees every line at once and starts a clean number.

The client declines

The instrument voids, its lines return to unreleased, and the schedule is exactly as it was. Ruling R1 decides whether declining part of an instrument is possible at all.

Stale checkpoint at release

Surfaced inline on the review sheet, never blocking. The instrument records which checkpoint was current when it was signed, so the question "did they see this plan?" is answerable years later.

The pattern  Every edge above resolves toward the same instinct: state it on the glass, record what was true, and make the recovery act loud rather than clever. Nothing here silently repairs itself, because a document that quietly changes what it said is worse than one that refuses to change at all.

Close · The ask22

Eight rulings.

The direction is drawn. These eight are the questions the drawing raised and could not answer by itself — each one changes a screen, and none of them changes the thesis.

R1 · partial signing

May a client decline part of an instrument? Decline-whole-and-supersede is simpler, honest, and slower. Per-line decline is kinder and doubles the state space.

R2 · acceptance

Who accepts finished trade work — the client, the designer, or both in sequence? It gates the balance invoice, so it gates the money.

R3 · bids in v1

Designer-recorded numbers, or requests sent from the portal? Recorded is a week; sent needs the coordination rails and a response surface.

R4 · progress billing

Do draws and holdbacks land in this build or a later one? Deposit-plus-balance covers most residential work. Anything larger will ask for draws.

R5 · allowance language

What exactly is a client agreeing to when they sign an allowance at its ceiling? The wording on the client document is a commitment, not a caption.

R6 · checkpoint staleness

What counts as drift? Any change, a percentage of the room, an absolute figure, or elapsed days. The strip is drawn for all four.

R7 · retiring the old path

When does the Drafting Room come out? With the first authorization shipped, or after a studio has walked the new ceremony once in production?

R8 · deposit default

Does the composition bar assume 50%, or read the deposit percent from the signed design-services agreement and show nothing until it can?

And then

Rule on R1–R8, and a build plan follows. Phase 1 is the second stamp track, the selection ceremony, linking execution, and the derived budget — the four screens in Act III, on a schedule that already exists. The trade scope is Phase 2. Nothing starts before the rulings.

A studio's records should read like a ledger kept by one careful hand, not like four hands writing the same number in four books and hoping.
