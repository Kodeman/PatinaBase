Folio 02

The client's objectives, restated as law

Five things you already decided.

Your own workflow presentation settled the model. It is a good model. This deck inherits it whole and spends its argument somewhere else — on how the model shows itself to a designer on a Tuesday.

I

Every stage has a gate, and every gate has a human accountable owner.

Not a status, not a percentage. A named person who owes an answer, and a moment where work is released or held.

II

Comments inform. One recorded approval releases work.

Conversation is not consent. The record separates the two, and only one of them moves the project.

III

Overdue is a condition — never silent approval.

Time passing changes how a thing looks. It never changes what a thing is. Nothing approves itself.

IV

"Ordered" is not a useful status.

Between the purchase and the room there are eleven things that can go wrong and one word covering all of them. That word is where the money is lost.

V

Patina is a lane, not a profession.

The platform carries a defined stretch of the work. It does not impersonate the designer, the architect, the maker, or the GC.

Everything that follows treats these as law. We do not relitigate the model.

Folio 03

The grammar of the product

The paper and the desk.

Two objects carry the whole product. Everything shipped since the dissolve is an inflection of one or the other, and every ruling in this deck is checked against them.

The Document. One engagement is one paper. It runs Brief → Discovery → Direction → Proposal → Project → Install → Care, and exactly one section is active at a time. Settled sections fold up and carry a seal — the fold is the archive, so history costs no screen. Work that needs a hand does not announce itself in a tray; it appears in the margin beside the line it concerns. The margin is the notification model. There is no other one.

The Desk. Two to four folios that need your hand today. Each carries one need line and one act — not a summary, not a chart, not a count. Projects that are moving without you appear as in-motion awareness and ask for nothing. There is no dashboard furniture on the Desk because a dashboard is what you build when you cannot say what the person should do next.

Why this grammar is a moat

A stage list is a week of work. A record that survives its own history is not.

Any competitor can ship eleven stages by Friday — the vocabulary is public, and most of it is RIBA with the serial numbers filed off. What cannot be retrofitted is a single object that holds the artifact, the question, the impact, and the name of the person who answered, all keyed to the same paper, from the first row forward. Bolt an approval log onto a project-management tool in year three and you get approvals that point at attachments nobody can prove. The grammar is the asset. The stages are vocabulary inside it.

Folio 04

The central tension

Two clocks on one wall.

A workflow model and a workflow display are different objects, and the wave under review conflates them.

The model is right: eleven canonical stages, hand-authored, versioned, mapped from real residential practice. It belongs in the platform and it is already there — in migration 00433, in get_project_workflow, in the derivation module. Nothing in this deck asks for a line of it back.

The display is the problem. The wave renders the eleven stages as a visible chronology inside a paper that already has a seven-section spine — and it does not confine itself to Project. The rail mounts unconditionally on every document. Inside Project it draws the project's own derivation. In Brief, Discovery, Direction, and Proposal it falls back to a per-section derivation and draws the same eleven rows against that. In Install and Care it draws them anyway, beneath the line This section has no explicit non-project workflow mapping.

So the second spine is not a Project-section problem to be fenced off. It is on every page of the paper. A designer opening any section reads two orderings of the same work, at the same time, on the same page — one that folds and seals, one that does not. Both are accurate. Both claim to say where the project is. Neither defers to the other. And in the two sections where the second spine has nothing to say, it says so at length rather than withdrawing.

That is not a rendering bug. It is a category error with a rendering symptom, and it is why four separate misalignments in folio 06 all read as the same complaint.

Folio 05

What Codex got right

Six decisions we would not have improved on.

This is not a rescue. The wave's judgment on the hard questions — the ones that are expensive to get wrong and invisible when you get them right — is sound. Each row below ships as authored.

Ships

Canonical stages are classification metadata, never a second state machine.

Stages label what already exists; they do not gate, transition, or own anything. Overdue is metadata only — computed, never stored as a state a row can get stuck in. This one decision is why the rest of the wave is recoverable by re-rendering rather than by rewriting.

Ships

The approval lifecycle is immutable by construction.

Artifact editions are frozen at publish. One decision lead is named and frozen with them. Three outcomes, no fourth. An approval expires only by studio withdrawal or by supersession — never by a timer. That last clause is objective III made into schema.

Ships

One single definition of "in the client's court."

Client attention resolves in one place and every surface reads that answer. No portal computes its own version. When the designer and the client disagree about whose turn it is, the record settles it without a meeting.

Ships

Stage-2 decisions are excluded from the legacy margin rail.

The new decision lane does not double-post into the old one. A designer never sees the same pending decision twice under two names. Restraint of exactly this kind is what keeps the margin trustworthy.

Ships

The client outcome copy carries no commercial policy.

Three sentences that describe what happens to the work, and say nothing about money, authority to purchase, or who holds funds. Given how unsettled the commercial questions are — see folio 14 — writing this copy without them was the right instinct and the right restraint.

Ships

The guide concept: one strip, one derived act, canonical anchors.

A single sentence about the project and a single act derived from it, anchored to the controls that already exist rather than to a new surface. The concept is right. Its six merged defects, in folio 06, are implementation — not architecture.

Folio 06

Where it is misaligned

Four misalignments. All presentation.

None of the four requires schema work. Every one of them is a decision about what appears on the glass, which is the cheapest kind of mistake a platform can make and the easiest to leave in place too long.

Mis 01

The second visible spine.

An eleven-row stage rail renders with its own numbering, its own status vocabulary, and its own detail pane — and not only inside Project. It mounts unconditionally on every document: Brief, Discovery, Direction, and Proposal each draw the full eleven rows against a per-section derivation, and Install and Care draw them beneath "This section has no explicit non-project workflow mapping." The paper now answers "where are we" twice on every page, and the two answers use different words for the same fact. A designer must learn which one the studio means — in all seven sections, including the two where the second spine has no answer to give.

Resolved by Ruling I · folio 07
Mis 02

Handoffs render as a separate band.

"Project handoffs" is a titled region carrying items that are, definitionally, things waiting on someone. That is exactly what the margin is. Two notification models on one paper means the designer must scan twice to know she is done — and a margin you can be done with is the entire promise of the Desk.

Resolved by Ruling III · folio 09
Mis 03

The approval ceremony is a standalone sub-document.

The most consequential moment in the product — the moment work is released — is authored on a 1,071-line sub-document with its own layout, its own state, and its own idea of what a decision looks like. It is not somewhere the designer navigates to; it mounts inside the paper, a second whole document grafted into the middle of the one she is already reading. (The navigated surface is the client's: a separate 349-line review page in the client portal.) A gate is a boundary ritual on the paper. Parking a standalone sub-document mid-page makes the hinge read as paperwork about the work — when the same six-part anatomy belongs at the section boundary, where the fold already is.

Resolved by Ruling II · folio 08
Mis 04

The merged guide carries six defects.

One: the derived act overrides on paused branches, so a paused project is told to "Add Working budget" instead of being told it is paused. Two: the Desk's entire morning read — six queries — gates guidance for every document, including documents that need none of it. Three: a Desk-wide error blanks guidance that never depended on the Desk in the first place. Four: when the Desk answers "nothing needs attention," that answer is discarded and silently replaced by one the page re-derives on its own — a wrong answer shown, not a missing one. Five: the guide's retry act carries a destination that looks like a real jump target but works only because one line of code recognises it by name — a second consumer honouring that destination would scroll instead of retrying. Six: the click-to-open guard checks a signal the margin's own buttons never emit, so the guide can toggle an already-open margin item shut on desktop.

Preconditions of Rulings V and VI · folio 11

Four symptoms, one cause: the wave designed a workflow display when the product needed the workflow to disappear into the paper it already had.

Folio 07

Ruling I · one paper, one spine

The stages become the derivation layer.

Keep the model. Retire the render. The eleven stages stop being something a designer reads and become something the paper knows.

Migration 00433 stays. get_project_workflow stays. The derivation module stays and grows. What goes is the visible rail: the eleven rows, the status vocabulary, the bracketed Project · stages 04–09 group, and the eleven-row definition list beside it. In their place the Project section bar carries one sub-label in mono — the stage, the track, and the position — and a Strata Mark whose three bands are the three live tracks.

The reduction is the argument. The mockup below is the same project, the same data, the same derivation, rendered twice. The second one is shorter because a designer does not need to be told the shape of the methodology every time she opens her own project. She needs to be told where she is.

Folio 08

Ruling II · the gate

A ceremony, not a form.

Approval is the moment the paper changes state. It should happen on the paper, at the boundary of the section it releases, and it should look like something being settled rather than something being submitted.

A gate has six parts and no more. Artifact — the exact thing, at a stated edition. Question — one sentence, answerable. Scope — what this releases and what it does not. Impact — the deltas, signed. Authority — the named lead, frozen at publish. Confirmation — one scored act. Five of the six already exist in the wave's approval schema, field for field: the artifact at its frozen edition, the question, the signed deltas, the authority snapshot, and the confirmation receipt. Scope is the exception — it has no dedicated field today, only a free-text context line doing the job by convention. That one field is the whole of what this ceremony asks of the schema. None of the six needs a separate surface to live on.

Drawn below as two stacked states so the eye watches the paper settle. State A is the boundary unfolded at the end of Direction. State B is the same boundary an hour later.

Folio 09

Ruling III · handoffs

The margin already is the band.

Dissolve the handoff band. Keep the projection behind it — whole, unchanged, and doing more work than it does today.

The 00441 and 00442 projection is a good read model: it resolves sender, recipient, current owner, expected response, due date, escalation state, and artifact provenance across two unlike sources into one shape. That is genuinely hard and it is done. What it does not need is a titled region of its own. Every row it produces is, by definition, a thing waiting on a named party — which is the exact definition of a margin item.

The four acts map one-to-one and keep their names: nudge, approve, redo, close. Stage provenance survives as microtext under the need line, where a designer can find it and does not have to read it.

Folio 10

Ruling IV · overdue

A condition changes how a thing looks.

Never what it is. Overdue is rendered in three places, computed in one, and stored nowhere.

The margin item takes a terracotta stamp. The guide's sentence changes tense — from a description of what is pending to a statement of how long it has been pending. The Desk reorders so the folio rises to first position. That is the whole mechanism, and it is enough: three quiet changes across three surfaces, each of which the designer was already looking at.

What we refuse is the entire genre of devices that make a person feel behind without helping them get ahead. A badge count is a number you cannot act on. A red banner is a shout with no verb. A push notification is an interruption that outlives its own urgency. Auto-approval is objective III violated in a single line of code. And a modal nag is a product asking a designer to acknowledge its feelings.

Folio 11

Rulings V and VI · the guide and the desk

Both key to the nearest open gate.

One rule, stated twice because it lands on two surfaces: the sentence a designer reads and the act she is offered both derive from the nearest open gate. Nothing else gets to decide.

Ruling V — the guide. The strip carries one sentence about the project's current gate and one act that resolves it. Six merged defects are preconditions, not follow-ups: the paused-branch override must go, so a paused project says it is paused; the Desk's entire morning read must stop gating guidance for documents that never needed Desk data; a Desk-wide error must degrade to one quiet note rather than blanking a strip that reads document-local state; the Desk's own "nothing needs attention" answer must survive instead of being discarded and re-derived locally; the retry act must stop depending on one line that recognises it by name; and the click-to-open guard must read a signal the margin actually emits, so the guide stops closing what it was asked to open.

Ruling VI — the Desk. Need lines derive from the same gate. "Marta's Direction approval has waited 6 days" is a gate rendered as a sentence — it names the person, the artifact, and the elapsed time, and its act is the one that moves it. The two-to-four folio ceiling is untouched. Studio Pulse reduces to a single aggregate sentence, because a designer wants to know the shape of her week in one line and then stop reading.

Folio 12

Ruling VII · procurement honesty

Closing the twenty-two per cent.

Stage 08 is the platform's worst self-score — 22% coverage, the only stage marked as a gap rather than a partial. It is also the stretch of the work where the most money and the most trust are lost.

The 22% is the client's own figure, from the coverage chart in the August 10 workflow blueprint — not a measurement taken in this repo. The wave's internal census reads the same stage from the other side and lands in the same place: its capability ledger dispositions stage 08 as Consolidate over rails that substantially exist already — trade RFQs and scopes, purchase orders, receiving and claims, the fulfilment ledger — and names the remedy as one item-level lifecycle and read contract. A self-score from outside and a census from inside agree on the direction. The item lifecycle is the missing operating detail.

"Ordered" is the whole problem in one word. It is true the moment an order leaves the studio's hands and stays true through production, freight, receiving, damage, claim, storage, and the six weeks a credenza sits in a warehouse while nobody asks about it. A designer reading "Ordered" learns that something happened once. She learns nothing about what is happening now, which is the only thing she can act on.

The answer is a stamp trail: fifteen numbered steps, three of them gates, rendered as marks on the line rather than a status field on a row. Settled steps carry a stamp. The live step carries one in clay. Future steps stand outlined and unstamped — visible, so the designer knows what is coming, and empty, so she knows it has not happened.

This is rendering grammar over the existing read model. It is expressly not a product-object schema proposal. The steps are how we draw what the orders book already knows; where a step needs a fact the book does not carry, that is a separate conversation with a separate cost, and it is not this one.

Folio 13

Ruling VIII · the client's side

The same paper, from the other chair.

Keep Codex's three outcomes and their copy verbatim. Add the ceremony. Import none of the pressure.

The client's surface owes three things the designer's does not. It must make immutability legible — "You are approving edition 3, exactly as shown" is not legal boilerplate, it is the sentence that makes an approval worth having six months later. It must state the impact in the same signed form the designer saw, so the two of them are looking at one number. And it must make holding the gate feel like a legitimate move rather than a failure to decide.

No overdue devices appear client-side. Elapsed time is a studio condition; it is the designer's job to nudge, and the record of that nudge belongs to the studio. A client who opens a paper and finds it counting days against them is a client who stops opening the paper. This ruling removes shipped behaviour, not a hypothetical — a terracotta "Overdue" renders on the client review surface today, set beside the due date, and it comes off. The condition itself is untouched: it stays the studio's, computed where it always was, and rendered on the three studio surfaces of folio 10.

Ruling IX · the co-approver, briefly

The second approver ships in schema and does not render until the governance ruling settles. Our proposed default when it does: a named witness on the seal, never a second gate. Two people who must both act is two ways to stall; one person who decides and one who is recorded as present is the same accountability with none of the deadlock. But that is a governance question about who may bind a household, and it is not ours to settle from a rendering deck.

Folio 14

Some of this deck's copy makes a claim the company has not yet decided it wants to make. Rather than quietly softening those lines until nobody notices the question, we stamp them.

A golden-hour stamp on a word means: this word is placeholder pigment. It renders, it is legible, it does not pretend. When the ruling lands, the stamp comes off and the word is set — once, in one place, by the decision rather than by an editor.
