Leah Session 05 · The Founders' Sit-Down

Everything Patina.
One table, two founders.

Five conversations in one sitting: her workflow, the MVP wedge, the money, the partners, the client's hands — and at the end, the story you tell together, with your faces on it.

Format~110 min · seven blocks
Session idL5 (on write-up)
CaptureType inline · Export notes below

Ground rules for yourself. Blocks 1–5 are an interview — she talks, you follow the energy, you don't pitch. Ask "walk me through the last time," never "would you ever." When she reacts to a proposal, chase the why behind the flinch. Block 6 flips the direction: that's the one place you pitch, then co-write. If a block runs hot, let it run — the signal buttons remember what got skipped.

0 · Arrival

5 min

Name what today is: not a status meeting, not a demo. You're borrowing her expertise for the four biggest open calls in the company, and then asking her to put her name on the front of it. Tell her the first hour is hers — you're mostly going to listen.

1 · The workflow, as it actually is

25 min

Goal: a time-and-pain map of one real project. Everything else in the session hangs off this. Don't accept generalities — anchor on a single recent project and walk it start to install.

1.1
Pick your most recent project. Walk me through it — first client contact to today. Where did the hours actually go?
Listen for phases she rushes past (comfortable) vs. phases she sighs at (the wedge lives there).
1.2
Of a typical ten-hour project week, how does it split — client communication, sourcing, documentation, chasing orders, actual design?
Get real numbers, even rough. This ratio is the MVP argument.
1.3
The blog draft has the two-hour retrace — hunting for the Vermont credenza you'd already found once. How often does that actually happen? What did it cost you last month?
Validates the founding pain with a frequency and a dollar figure.
1.4
Which tool in your stack would you refuse to give up? Which one do you resent every time you open it?
The refused one is the bar Patina must clear; the resented one is the opening.
1.5
Where do projects stall — and when they stall, what is the client doing?
Stall points are where a client experience earns its keep (Block 5 callback).
Block signal

2 · The MVP wedge

15 min

Goal: force a choice. Patina today spans the capture extension, the Aesthete Engine, the Designer Portal (the Document), and a consumer iOS app. She doesn't get to say "all of it."

2.1
If Patina could do exactly one thing perfectly by launch, which: never lose a piece again (capture + memory) · recommendations that actually match taste · a client-facing experience that makes you look like a bigger studio · procurement that runs itself?
Make her rank all four, not pick one. The gap between #1 and #2 matters.
2.2
Which of those four would make you text a designer friend about Patina, unprompted?
Growth wedge ≠ utility wedge. If they differ, that's a finding.
2.3
What would you pay for today, this month, as a working designer — and what would you expect free forever?
Her free/paid line previews every designer's. Feeds Block 3.
Block signal

3 · The money

15 min

Goal: stress-test the v4 model against a working designer's reality. Don't present the whole deck — read each proposal aloud, watch her face, chase the flinch.

Proposal 1 · The project fee
The client pays a 4% platform fee on total project value, line-itemed on the invoice. A $30K refresh pays $1,200; a $250K home pays $10K. The designer never pays it.
v4 advisor recommendation · Stream 02
3.1
Would your clients accept that line item? At what project size does it break — and what would the platform have to visibly do to justify it?
Proposal 2 · The 25% Pledge
A quarter of every marketplace commission dollar goes back to designers as teaching royalties — contractual, in the ToS, forever. Paid for curation, maker sourcing, style teaching, reviews.
v4 advisor recommendation · Stream 03
3.2
Honestly: would you teach for that? Would the designers you know? Or is the tool itself the payment and the royalty a distraction?
3.3
18% blended take on maker sales, built on trade pricing. You hold those vendor relationships today — does that number survive contact with them?
3.4
How do you charge now — flat, hourly, percentage of FF&E? Where does Patina layer in without eating your own margin?
Block signal

4 · The partners

10 min

Goal: define "Patina-grade" and the partnership shape. Ten Midwest heritage makers are already seeded in the Vendor Pipeline — use them as the concrete test.

4.1
What makes a maker Patina-grade? Name a maker you'd stake your name on, and one you'd never let near the catalog — what separates them?
4.2
What do those makers actually want that they don't get from reps, showrooms, or 1stDibs?
The recruiting pitch for the supply side, in her words.
4.3
Mechanically: who holds the trade account, who ships, who eats a damaged piece? What's the minimum a partnership agreement must nail down?
4.4
Beyond makers — photographers, workrooms, GCs, receivers. Which adjacent partner would change your projects most?
Block signal

5 · The client's hands

15 min

Goal: design the client experience from her clients' actual behavior. Mobile and web are different rooms — figure out which moments belong in which.

5.1
What does a client of yours see today — emails, PDFs, a Pinterest board? Where do they get confused, and where do they get anxious?
5.2
Take the big moments — first concept reveal, approvals, the long wait on lead times, install day. What should each one feel like, and which have you seen done beautifully anywhere?
5.3
Which of those moments belong on their phone, and which deserve a laptop and a glass of wine? Would your clients actually scan their own rooms?
Splits the mobile/web surface honestly; tests the AR assumption with real clients.
5.4
Finish this sentence: a client should close Patina feeling ______.
Block signal

6 · The story, with faces on it

15 min

Direction flips: now you pitch. The position — Patina is a crafted product made by two people whose names and faces are on it, exactly like the pieces it champions. Handmade software, Madison, Wisconsin. Designer-taught, never "AI-powered." Then read her back her own words:

From her draft · "Why We're Building Patina"
"That's why Kody and I started Patina. Not because we wanted to build another furniture marketplace, but because we wanted to build something that actually fits the way designers' minds work… a tool that gets better with time, that learns from every piece you save."
Strata · Content/Blog-01 · with her byline: co-founder & design director, Madison, WI
6.1
You wrote this months ago. Does it still feel true? What would you change today?
6.2
Faces on the product — where's your comfort line? Name and bio · photo on the site · founder video · a recurring voice (blog, short video, teaching notes). Which cadence could you actually sustain?
Get a real commitment level, not enthusiasm. Undersell and overdeliver beats the reverse.
6.3
The origin, in fact-check form: how do we tell the story of the two of us — how it started, why Madison, why "patina"? What's the one detail that must be in it, and anything off-limits?
6.4
Three years out — what do you want to be known for, personally, because Patina existed?
Block signal
