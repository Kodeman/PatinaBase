Row vocabulary

A phase holds three row types, each with its own voice: milestones (diamonds — sign-offs, decisions, deliveries, with stamps), items (the chips you already ship — sign-off, punch — with ball-in-court inline), and threads (parallel work like procurement, rendered as a running stitch, never forced into sequence).

State through weight

Closed phases compress to one light line — history in a whisper. The active phase opens fully in bold. Future phases show heading, target, and dependencies in muted Aged Oak. The dashed spine below today mirrors the dashed future of the old bar — a vocabulary your users already learned.

Actions on hover, invisible until needed

Hovering a phase heading reveals + Item, + Milestone, Edit dates — quiet mono links, no permanent action bar competing with content. On touch, a long-press on the heading opens the same three.

The anchor

Any phase or milestone can be anchored — pinned to a hard date. Anchored entries wear a charcoal chip and hold their ground when upstream phases move. Install day is the canonical anchor: it's usually promised before the design is finished.

The vision

The project reads top to bottom like a story — and every open question in the project lives inside the chapter where it belongs.

Read: the glance

Weight tells status, the strong vertical rule tells today, diamonds tell commitments, the parallel hairline tells the truth about overlap. Nothing truncates — labels sit above the line at natural width, staggered when they'd collide.

Navigate: the minimap

Click a phase label → the spine scrolls to it and unfolds. Click a diamond → the spine opens that milestone's phase with the row highlighted. The rule is a table of contents drawn as an instrument.

Edit: the time surface

Phase boundaries are handles — drag to re-date, and the ripple previews live (slide 8). Milestones drag along the line, snapping to days. Anchored entries refuse the drag with a firm nudge: "Install is anchored — unpin to move it."

Pin behavior

At rest, the rule sits where the current bar sits. On scroll, it pins beneath the project title at reduced height — labels fold into the line, diamonds and today remain. The glance survives every scroll depth.

The vision

Read in the spine. Adjust on the rule. The schedule as an instrument you play, not a report you receive.

Building the Schedule · I · Where schedules come from

The schedule is born in the proposal — and never rebuilt.

Proposals and projects are already the same data structure in two views. The schedule honors that: the phase chain a designer drafts in the proposal becomes the live project schedule when the client signs. The signature cuts baseline v1 — the promise the rest of the project is measured against.

Backward from the anchor

Most projects know their install date before they know their plan — the client booked the party first. Anchor install day, and the chain computes backward: each phase's start derived from its duration and its successor. What's left over renders as slack — or as a Terracotta warning that the chain doesn't fit.

Forward from today

No hard date? The chain computes forward from the signature date. Either way, the designer enters durations, not arithmetic — the schedule does the math and shows its work in the meta line.

The vision

A designer never faces an empty calendar grid. She faces her own standards, her own history, or a single ghost line — and a chain that does the arithmetic.

The chain model

Every phase is a duration plus a link — "4 weeks, following Schematic Design." Dates are derived by resolving the chain. This is what makes the schedule adaptable: a 3-phase refresh and a 7-phase renovation are the same machine with different links, and re-dating one thing flows through everything unanchored.

Natural entry

Duration fields accept the way people talk: 3w, 10d, Sep 21. Typing a hard date anchors the entry automatically — the chip appears, and the designer can unpin it with one click. No date-picker safari.

Overlap is legal

Set a phase to follow an earlier link — or drag its start before its predecessor's end on the rule — and it doesn't snap back or throw an error. It drops to a parallel thread, the procurement pattern. The schedule permits what reality insists on.

Milestones ride phases

Milestones live inside phases as offsets ("3 days before phase end") or anchored dates. Four kinds — Sign-off, Decision, Delivery, Event — each already speaking the stamp language from the proposal. When a phase moves, its milestones ride along; anchored ones hold.

The vision

The designer describes the work. The schedule does the arithmetic — and shows it, in the meta line, in plain sight.

The ripple, previewed

Grab a boundary handle and drag: ghost ticks render every downstream consequence in dashed Terracotta — new dates, sliding milestones, shrinking slack — while the committed schedule stays solid beneath. Release, and the confirm strip states the change in one honest sentence.

Commit or revert — never silent

Nothing moves until Commit. Esc reverts. Construction discipline: a schedule change is a decision, not a gesture. Every commit writes a revision entry (slide 9) with who, what, and why.

Anchors push back

The ripple flows around anchors. If a drag would break one — the chain no longer fits before install — the anchor doesn't move; the conflict renders in Terracotta and the confirm strip names it. The designer chooses: absorb the slack, overlap a phase, or consciously unpin.

Edits in the spine

The same model, without the drag: click any date in a phase's meta line and it becomes a field — type +5d or Jul 29 — the ripple previews inline in the meta lines of downstream phases before commit. Words in the spine, time on the rule, one grammar.

The vision

The schedule never surprises its owner. Consequences first, commitment second — the opposite of every tool that reflows silently and apologizes never.

The Signature Move · Baselines, ghosts, and honest history

A schedule that wears its history.

When the client signs, the schedule's dates freeze as baseline v1 — the promise. From then on, every committed change cuts a revision. Where current dates differ from the baseline, faint Clay ghosts mark where the promise stood. The brand is "Where Time Adds Value." Even the schedule earns a patina.

Why baselines matter

Construction learned this decades ago: a schedule without a baseline is a story that keeps rewriting itself. The baseline is what makes a slip a fact instead of a feeling — and what makes held dates a visible achievement.

Why it's a brand move

The 25% Pledge runs on a public ledger because transparency is the credibility engine. The schedule runs on the same instinct: revisions carry a reason and a name. A designer who shows her schedule's history is a designer a client trusts with the next date.

The vision

Where time adds value — even here. The schedule doesn't hide its scars; it numbers them, names them, and holds the line anyway.

Open Questions · The next rulings this direction needs

Four questions before Slice 01 cuts.

1

Client visibility, now or later?

Does the client see a filtered spine in Slice 01 — studio rows hidden — or does the client-facing schedule wait for the Almanac projection? Affects Slice 01's scope directly.

2

Do clients see the ghosts?

The baseline and revision ledger: full transparency (the brand argument — it's the Pledge instinct) or studio eyes only (the comfort argument)? A middle path: clients see revisions that touched client-facing dates.

3

How do items sort inside a phase?

The old Coordination section grouped by ball-in-court. Inside a phase entry: keep court grouping, sort by due date, or blocking-first? Blocking-first is the exception-first instinct.

4

How much schedule does the proposal show?

Full chain with milestones — the client signs against specific sign-off dates — or phases only, with milestones composed after signature? The baseline is only as strong as what it froze.
