Act I · Cold open02

Two people, same morning, both hunting a phone number.

The designer, 8:40 a.m.

The tile is landing Thursday and the sub needs the layout. She knows his name. She scrolls four months of texts, finds a thread about a different house, and copies the number out of it. Patina holds the tile spec, the room scan, the PO, and the install date. It does not hold the man who sets the tile.

The founder, 9:15 a.m.

Her senior designer is out for two weeks and someone has to reach the drapery workroom. The workroom exists — in that designer's saved vendors, under her login. The studio paid for those relationships across six years of projects and cannot see them.

The rolodex is the most valuable thing a studio owns, and right now it lives in a phone.

The problem in one line
Why this is urgent now

Studio workspaces shipped. Studios are no longer one designer — they are two, five, nine people sharing projects. The moment a second person joins, "who is on this job" stops being obvious and starts being a question the software has to answer.

Act I · The gap04

Three missing pieces.

Each one is small. Together they are the difference between a login and a studio.

Missing · one

Staff titles

Patina knows Leah is a member. It does not know she is a Senior Designer. Permission is not a job. Without the title, the software cannot route the right work to the right desk, address a person by what they do, or print a crew list anyone recognizes.

Missing · two

A studio rolodex

There is no studio-scoped list of people and companies the studio works with. Contacts are stranded inside individual projects, or inside one designer's saved vendors, and never accumulate into anything the studio owns.

Missing · three

A roster surface

No single screen answers "who is on this job." Staff, client, and trades live in three different places, and two of the three have no UI at all. The question everyone asks first is the one we do not answer.

Act I · Thesis05

A studio is a crew.
A project is a production.
Patina should know
the call sheet.

A film set does not rebuild its crew from memory every morning. It keeps a rolodex of people it trusts, and every production draws a call sheet from it — this job, these names, these numbers, this week.

The permanent crew

Your studio. Titled, tiered, and the same on every job. Hired once, not re-entered per project.

The rolodex

Everyone the studio has ever worked with. Built by working, not by data entry. It outlives any one project — and any one employee.

The call sheet

This project's crew, drawn from the rolodex in a few taps. Names, roles, trades, and how to reach each one.

Act II · The cast06

Two questions, two systems. Keep them apart.

Every staffing model that fails collapses these into one list. They are not one list.

Question one · exists today

Permission tier — what you can touch

Owner, admin, member, guest. Four values, enforced in row-level security, already shipped and already guarded by migration 00319. This is a security decision and it stays exactly as coarse as it is.

Question two · proposed

Job title — what you do

Principal, Senior Designer, Procurement, Bookkeeper. Descriptive, editable, and the thing Patina uses to route work, address people, and print a roster a human recognizes. Never consulted by a policy.

A Bookkeeper outranks a Senior Designer on invoices and sits below her on everything else. Collapse the two dimensions and one of those facts becomes a lie.

Why they stay orthogonal
Titles do

Suggest a tier at invite time. Label the roster. Route site requests, approvals, and notifications by role.

Titles never

Appear in an RLS policy, gate a route, or change what a query returns. Retitling someone is a cosmetic act.

The escape hatch

Nine curated titles plus free text. A studio that calls the job something else types it and moves on.

Act II · Staff07

Nine titles that cover a real studio.

Drawn from how interior design firms actually staff. The tier column is a suggestion offered at invite time — pre-selected, always editable, never enforced.

Plus one more

Custom. A free-text title alongside the nine. Same storage, no validation beyond length. "Studio Coordinator", "Head of Procurement", "Partner" — a studio's own word for a job is not an error condition.

Act II · The cast09

Who is actually on a job.

Three sides. A real residential project runs twelve to twenty names across them, and today Patina holds a third of that.

Studio side

Your people, assigned

  • Lead designer — owns the job
  • Support designer — one or several
  • Project manager — schedule and site
  • Procurement — POs and receiving
  • Bookkeeper — invoices only

Drawn from staff. Everyone here has a Patina account already.

Client side

The household, in full

  • Client — the portal account
  • Spouse or partner — approves half the decisions
  • Family office — pays the invoices
  • Assistant — schedules the walkthroughs
  • Property manager — holds the keys

One client, several people. Today we model the first and lose the rest.

Build & supply side

Everyone who touches the house

  • Architect · General contractor
  • Superintendent / GC's PM
  • Subs, by trade
  • Vendors & showrooms, by specialty
  • Installers · Receivers
  • Photographer · Stager

Partly modeled today: GC, sub, installer, receiver.

New party kinds

Four additions to the existing four: architect, photographer, stager, and client — so the client side of a roster stops being a special case bolted on beside it.

Act III · Setup11

Day one, from the owner's chair.

Five steps. Two of them already exist. None of them takes more than a minute, and step four is allowed to be skipped.

1

Name and brand the studio exists today

Workspace, logo, colors. Unchanged — this is the flow that shipped with studio workspaces.

2

Set your own title new

One picker, defaulted to Principal because you own the workspace. The first place titles show their face.

3

Invite your crew extends the invite

Email, name, title. The title pre-selects a permission tier; you can override it before sending. Same invitation email, same acceptance flow, one more field on the form.

4

Seed the rolodex — or don't new

Three doors: promote the vendors and project contacts you already have into the studio rolodex through a review step; import a CSV; or skip. Skipping is the honest default — the rolodex fills itself from the next four projects whether you seed it or not.

5

Start the first project exists today

Which is where the call sheet begins, and where every name you add starts earning its way into the rolodex.

The discipline

No empty-state homework. A studio that does nothing on day one still ends up with a full rolodex — it just gets there by working instead of by typing.

Act III · Kickoff13

Staffing a new production.

The roster fills in the order a designer actually thinks: my people, then the client, then the trades.

1

Create the project exists today

Name, address, scope. Nothing about this step changes.

2

Studio side auto-fills

Whoever created the project lands as lead. Add support designers, a project manager, procurement, and a bookkeeper from a staff typeahead — the list is short and already known, so this is three taps, not a form.

3

Attach the client

Pick from the CRM or add inline. The same act grants portal access through the policies that already exist — attaching a client has always been a permission, and now it is also a roster row. Client reps follow: spouse, family office, assistant.

4

Build and supply side

Architect, GC, superintendent, subs by trade, vendors by specialty, installers, receivers. From the rolodex when the studio has one; inline when it doesn't — and either way the entry ends up in the rolodex.

By the end of kickoff the project can answer "who do I call about the tile" — which is the question, on every job, every week.

Act III · Adding15

Two ways onto the call sheet.

One for the person you have used before, one for the person you just met. Both end in the same place.

Path one

From the rolodex

Search the studio's people by what you need, not by what you remember. Subs → tile. Vendors → drapery workroom. Anyone we used on the Braddock house.

One tap carries the whole entry across: contact details, trade or specialty, and the history — six projects, last used in March.

SubstileVendorsInstallers
Path two

New person, inline

The add-person flow that already exists on project parties, with a trade or specialty picker attached. Name, company, phone, email, role.

At save: "Add to studio rolodex" — checked by default. That single checkbox is the whole flywheel. Uncheck it and the person stays on this job only.

The rule

The roster row keeps its own snapshot of name, phone, and email as entered. Editing the rolodex entry next year never rewrites what this project's history says.

Why the checkbox is pre-checked

Because the default should be the behavior that pays. A studio that never thinks about the rolodex still builds one — and the designer who does think about it can opt a one-off out in a single click.

Act III · Compounding16

Every project makes the next one faster.

The rolodex is not a data-entry chore that pays off later. It is a byproduct of doing the work, and it compounds without anyone maintaining it.

Month one

An empty rolodex and a roster you fill by hand. Roughly what a designer does today, minus the scrolling.

Month six

Most of the trades on a new job are already in the list. Kickoff staffing drops from an afternoon to ten minutes.

Year one

The rolodex is the studio's institutional memory of who is good — and it survives the employee who built it.

Act IV · Why it pays17

Five rails already built, all waiting on a roster.

This is the argument for doing it now. Every one of these is shipped or dormant — none needs new infrastructure, all of them need to know who is on the job.

Site requests & field linksshipped

The roster becomes the picker — "send the tile sub the site request" turns into a name you tap, not a phone number you hunt for.

SMSbuilt · dormant until Twilio

The roster is both the consent ledger and the audience list, ready the day 10DLC clears.

Purchase ordersshipped

The vendor picker goes roster-first, rolodex-second, global catalog third — nearest relationship wins.

Client portalshipped

Attaching a client to the roster is the access grant, through the row-level policies already in production.

Coordination courtsshipped

Address a project by role instead of by person: notify the GC and everyone on electrical, and let the roster resolve the names.

The shape of the bet

We are not proposing five new features. We are proposing the missing input to five features that already exist.

Act IV · Reach18

Three ways to be on a call sheet.

A roster is not a list of users. Most of the people on a job will never see a login screen, and the design has to be at peace with that.

Reach state · one

Full account

Staff and clients. Email, password or magic link, a portal, a session. These are the people who live in Patina every week.

Roughly a quarter of a typical roster.

Reach state · two

Field link

GCs, subs, installers, receivers. A login-less magic link that opens exactly the site request or delivery they need — already built, already in production.

The rail nobody else in this category has.

Reach state · three

Contact only

The architect, the photographer, the property manager, the workroom. A name, a number, an email, and a place on the sheet. No account, ever, unless they ask.

The majority.

Most of these people will never log in. That's the feature — a tool the trades have to sign up for is a tool the trades ignore.

The design constraint stated plainly

One new table, two columns on an existing one, one new foreign key, one revived table, and a view that unions the halves. Everything else is surface.

Act IV · Discipline20

What we're not building — and the order we build the rest.

Not this

A vendor portal

Accounts for workrooms and showrooms. Field links already reach the people who need reaching, without asking a supplier to adopt our software.

Not this

Per-title RLS

Nine titles times ten capabilities is ninety support questions. Titles describe; tiers enforce.

Not this

One merged people table

Auth-bearing people and phone-first contacts have different lifecycles. They stay separate underneath and look like one list on the surface.

Not this

Taxonomy lookup tables

Trades and specialties stay code-resident vocabulary over a TEXT column — the FieldTrade precedent. Adding a word should not need a migration.

Not this

SMS activation

Twilio stays dormant. The roster prepares the consent ledger; it does not turn on the sending.

And to be clear

Nothing here is built

No migration, no table, no route, no component. This is a direction to approve or redirect, not a status report.

Build sequence — lowest risk first

Phase 1

Staff titles

Two additive columns, a title picker in the invite, titles on the people screen.

Ships alone and is useful alone. Touches no policy.

Phase 2

The rolodex

studio_contacts, the specialties vocabulary, and a rolodex tab on the studio's People room.

New table, new policies, nothing existing rewritten.

Phase 3

Roster wiring

The call sheet, add-from-rolodex, promote-to-rolodex, the new party kinds, v_project_roster.

The phase a designer feels. Rides phases 1 and 2.

Phase 4

Studio directory

people_directory rescoped from designer to studio, with a Mine / Studio filter.

Widest blast radius. Deliberately last, once the rest is proven.

Why this order

Each phase is worth shipping on its own, and each one is a strictly smaller risk than the one after it. Phase 1 could go out next week and would already answer "who is the project manager here."

Close · The ask21

Six rulings, then a green light.

None of these are engineering questions. All six are yours, and the first phase cannot be scoped honestly without at least R1 and R2.

Needs a ruling · R1

Rolodex visibility. Fully shared studio-wide, or does each contact carry a "private to me" flag for designers who protect their sources?

Needs a ruling · R2

Member project visibility. Do members see every project in the studio, or only the ones they are assigned to? This one changes a policy either way.

Needs a ruling · R3

GC reach ceiling. Field-link-forever, or are general contractor accounts on the roadmap? The answer decides how much we invest in the link.

Needs a ruling · R4

Client-visible roster. Show the call sheet in the client portal — transparency sells — or keep it back-of-house?

Needs a ruling · R5

Migration consent. Do a designer's existing contacts fold into the studio rolodex automatically, or does every one of them go through a review step?

Needs a ruling · R6

Seats and billing. Is staff count a pricing dimension now, or free until we have traction? Phase 1 is where seats become countable.

The ask

Rule on these six, then green-light Phase 1.

Two additive columns and a title picker. No policy rewritten, no existing screen changed, and nothing that has to be undone if a later phase goes a different way.

Where this lands

We already hold the spec, the room, the purchase order, and the install date. What we do not hold is the crew — and the crew is what a studio is actually made of. A studio's real asset is the people it can call. Right now we are asking it to keep them somewhere else.
