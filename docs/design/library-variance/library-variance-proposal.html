The Library
Learns Variance

How Patina handles the many forms a piece of furniture takes — sizes, woods, grades, COM, sectionals, and the cabinet that doesn't exist yet.

"Which bed did we actually buy?"
Product proposal · for team review Status proposal — nothing built Library head migration 00380 August 2026
The problem02

One row per thing. Furniture doesn't work that way.

A Piece in our library is a single flat record: one price pair, one dimensions blob, one finish string, one lead_time_weeks integer. It describes a photograph of one configuration.

What the row holds

One price. One image set. One set of dimensions. One lead time. One vendor SKU.

What the piece actually is

A bed in four sizes. A table in six woods. A sofa across a fabric-grade ladder, or in the client's own cloth.

Where the difference goes

Into a notes field, a hand-made column, a second near-identical row, or an email the system never sees.

The whole gap in one line

Everything a designer decides after picking a piece — size, wood, grade, yardage, side mark — has nowhere structured to live, and nothing carries it to the vendor.

Verified against the schema: no variant, option, axis, or SKU-matrix table exists anywhere in the database. products.finishes[] is written and read by nothing. pricing_tiers has neither readers nor writers. The consolidated Library PRD does not mention variants, sizes, sectionals, or custom work at all — variance is not currently a tracked gap.

Why now03

We already shipped half the answer.

Spec Books (July 2026, migration 00380) solved master-versus-selection. project_ffe_specs sits 1:1 with an FF&E line and holds the project's own sku, finish, material, color_fabric, selected_dimensions. Precedence is frozen. Project edits never touch the master.

"One chair can serve five projects and still require five different finishes."

Shipped thesis — Spec Books program, docs/design/spec-books/contracts.md
The half that shipped

The project can now say which finish it chose. Free-text fields, a four-tier resolution chain, N/A declarations, source verification, audience allow-lists.

The half that didn't

The chair still doesn't know it comes in five finishes. There is no offer to choose from — only a blank box to retype, project after project.

The competitive read

Programa has no variant model either — free-text color / finish / material plus unlimited custom fields. This is open whitespace, not a gap we're behind on. Option selection, configurable assemblies, and COM/COL are unmodelled across the category.

Ingest is already ahead of the schema

The Chrome extension and the catalog-feed normalizer both already parse finishes, dimensions, and materials with confidence scores. They extract structure, then throw it away because there's nowhere to put it.

Methods note — read this before the quotes

These five interviews are PM-synthesized composites, not transcripts. No designer sat for these sessions. Each narrative is written to the house interview instrument and grounded in workarounds observable in the product and the repo — every archetype's "what they do today" cites a real surface, not a remembered anecdote.

Read the quotes as hypotheses to confirm, starting with Leah at Middlewest — our pilot designer and first daily user. The evidence slide that follows is the part that is not synthesized.

Interview 0105

The sofa is ordered twice.

Studio principal, full-service residential. Her upholstery specs are the most consequential thing she writes and the least structured thing in the system. A grade-C sofa is one order. The client's own fabric is a second order, to a second vendor, with its own yardage, lead time, ship-to, and side mark.

"COM isn't a note. It's a purchase from a second vendor with its own lead time — and I'm typing it into the same box where I put 'ask about the leg finish.'"

Composite · studio principal synthesized

"The library knows the sofa. It doesn't know which sofa I bought."

Composite · studio principal synthesized
What she does today — observed

COM details live in the FF&E line's free-text notes field. The placeholder tells her to: "Specification notes, lead time, COM details…"

Fabric grade becomes a hand-made per-project column. Nailhead, welt, and railroading go in the same paragraph as the reminder to call the rep.

Ranked need

1. COM as a structured thing that reaches the vendor.
2. Fabric grade as a real ladder with price deltas, not a retyped word.
3. A spec that survives from her book to the PO without her rewriting it.

Interview 0206

She clipped the bed four times.

Solo designer, high volume of stocked goods. Beds, tables, and case goods that ship from a shelf in a size × finish matrix. Capture speed is sacred: she is standing in a showroom or on a vendor site with a client waiting.

"I clipped the bed four times because I needed four sizes. Then the app told me I had four duplicates and asked which one to delete."

Composite · solo designer, stocked goods synthesized

"Don't make me configure at capture. Two seconds, or I stop using it."

Composite · solo designer, stocked goods synthesized
What she does today — observed

The extension reads the vendor's variant picker, flattens it into available_colors[], and pins colors[0] to whatever happened to be on screen. One capture of a bed PDP is one product, silently locked to one size.

So the same bed enters the library N times — and the duplicate-detection panel, which merges image-identical products, reads a size matrix as a mess to clean up.

Ranked need

1. Capture stays one tap. Nothing new to answer.
2. The sizes the vendor already published get kept, not discarded.
3. One Piece she can return to, instead of four rows she has to tell apart.

Interview 0307

The real purchase order is an email.

Procurement-heavy designer / ops lead. She owns the paper. Every order she sends is followed by a message carrying the parts the PO left out, and every lead-time promise she makes is a guess about a finish, not a product.

"The PO says 'Halsted Dining Table, 1, $2,400.' The vendor emailed back asking which top. That email is the actual purchase order."

Composite · procurement lead synthesized

"Lead time isn't a number on the product. It's a number on the finish."

Composite · procurement lead synthesized
What she does today — observed

The generated PO PDF renders name, quantity, price. Nothing else. No finish, no size, no SKU, no COM. The spec-book fields never reach purchase_orders or fulfillment at all.

Option-level lead time has no home: lead_time_weeks is one integer on the master. Substitutions get caught after the fact, in the Back-of-House exception flow.

Ranked need

1. The PO carries the spec. Everything else is second.
2. Lead time that moves with the option chosen.
3. A record of what was ordered that still reads correctly six months later.

Interview 0408

Half her project isn't in the library, because it isn't anywhere yet.

Custom / casework-heavy designer. Built-ins, millwork, a banquette cut to a bay window. Nothing is picked. Everything is drawn, quoted by a shop, revised, sampled, and approved.

"Half my project doesn't exist in the library, because it doesn't exist anywhere yet. I'm drawing it."

Composite · casework designer synthesized

"The allowance is a placeholder for a piece I'll eventually describe in five paragraphs. Right now those paragraphs live in an email thread with the millworker."

Composite · casework designer synthesized
What she does today — observed

An item_type = 'allowance' line with a budget min/max — the only price-range primitive in the system — holds the money until the shop quotes. Then the piece becomes an FF&E line with product_id = NULL: no spec surface, no images, no Piece room.

There is no is_custom flag, no fabrication order, no shop drawing, no approval-sample loop. ProductionModel exists only on vendors, as a market descriptor.

Ranked need

1. Turn an allowance into a real piece that can hold a spec and a drawing.
2. A quoted price that replaces the range without losing the range's history.
3. The approval loop — sample, sign-off, revision — on the record instead of in mail.

Interview 0509

She retypes "plus $340, plus two weeks" into every project.

E-design / high-volume presentation. Her product is the choice she puts in front of a client. Two beds, two woods, a clear price difference, a fast yes.

"I retype 'plus $340, plus two weeks' into every project. It's the same table."

Composite · e-design designer synthesized

"My client wants to see the walnut next to the oak. I want to show them one piece with two swatches, not two pieces."

Composite · e-design designer synthesized
What she does today — observed

"Queen vs King" is built as two separate decision options with hand-typed Cost Delta and Lead-Time Delta strings. It is per-decision and never reusable; the client sees two cards and a ±$ pill.

In the spec book, selected_dimensions is edited as a raw JSON textarea. There is no price range anywhere — no "from $2,400" on a piece that starts at $2,400.

Ranked need

1. Compare across an axis — derive the option cards from the piece.
2. Deltas that come from the vendor's price list, once.
3. Swatches the client can see, not adjectives they have to trust.

Evidence10

The workaround museum.

Not synthesized. Each of these is in the product right now, and together they are the strongest signal we have: designers already built variance by hand, using whatever was nearest.

designer-portal · spec-fields-manager placeholder: "e.g. Finish"

Per-project custom columns are text / number / url only — no select kind. The key generator already anticipates the collision: finish, finish_2. Designers are hand-rolling a variant schema, one project at a time, and hitting name collisions doing it.

designer-portal · ffe-item-forms placeholder: "Specification notes, lead time, COM details…"

Customer's own material — a second vendor, a yardage, a ship-to — lives in a notes box, because the placeholder is where the product told her to put it.

designer-portal · PO generate route line: name · qty · price

The vendor-facing PDF renders three fields. No finish, no size, no SKU, no COM. project_ffe_specs never reaches purchase_orders.

spec book · SelectionEditor <textarea> — selected_dimensions (raw JSON)

Finish, material, and color/fabric render as plain text inputs; dimensions are typed as JSON by hand. This is a designer surface asking for a data structure.

extension · capture [class*="variant"] → available_colors[]

We already scrape the vendor's variant picker, then flatten it and pin colors[0] to whatever was on screen. Structure in, string out.

admin-portal · duplicate-detection-panel merge image-identical products

The only duplication machinery we own removes exactly what a size matrix produces. The system currently classifies variance as an error.

designer-portal · DecisionOptionBuilder Cost Delta · Lead-Time Delta (free text)

The closest thing to a working variant UI is per-decision, hand-typed, and thrown away at the end of the project.

packages/types + admin-portal · variant-editor Variant { sku, options, price } — no table

Dead scaffold, not a head start. A complete SKU/options/CSV editor whose save path enumerates 15 columns and silently discards variants; use-variants.ts calls routes that 404, against a catalog service that does not exist. Ruled a phantom field in R70 and deferred: expose only columns that genuinely exist.

public.products finishes[] · pricing_tiers · available_colors[]

Three variance-shaped columns with no consumers. finishes[] is written and never read. pricing_tiers has neither. We have been leaving ourselves notes in the schema.

Framing11

Five kinds of variance. Two data shapes.

Ordered by how much is decided before the designer arrives — from a shelf you pick off, to a drawing that doesn't exist until someone makes it.

Class 01

Stocked variant

The maker holds inventory across a finite matrix. Queen or King. Oak or walnut. Ships from a shelf.

→ optioned

Class 02

Configured option

Made to order along finite axes. Twelve finishes, four woods, a fabric-grade ladder, a base choice.

→ optioned

Class 03

COM / COL

The client supplies the material. Two vendors, two lead times, a yardage, a ship-to, a side mark.

→ optioned + com_details

Class 04

Modular assembly

A sectional. One piece in the room, five components on the truck, one line on the schedule.

→ kit

Class 05

Bespoke

Drawn, not picked. Casework, built-ins, a banquette. There is no catalog row until we make one.

→ bespoke

The insight the model rests on

Classes 01 and 02 are the same data shape — axes with values, values with deltas. Whether the maker builds it or ships it from a shelf is a fulfillment property, not a modeling one. Model them once and two thirds of the problem is done.

Principles12

Six rules the design has to obey.

Every one of these came out of the interviews or out of an invariant we already froze. They are the constraints, not the aspirations.

Rule 01

Capture stays instant

One tap in a showroom, same as today. Nothing about options gates a save, ever. A piece with no axes is a complete piece.

Rule 02

Structure is progressive

Options are an upgrade you apply to a row that already exists — never a precondition for creating one.

Rule 03

Products stay canonical

A project selection never writes to the master. This is the invariant 00380 froze, and nothing here bends it.

Rule 04

Free text stays the render source

A structured selection fills in the existing spec fields. Every shipped spec-book surface keeps reading exactly what it reads today.

Rule 05

The spec must reach the PO

A selection that never leaves the app isn't a selection. If the vendor can't read it on paper, we didn't build it.

Rule 06

"Decide later" is a real answer

Every axis is skippable. Unconfigured is a legitimate state; readiness carries the debt instead of a modal blocking the work.

The snapshot14

A selection is a snapshot, not a pointer.

project_ffe_specs gains selected_options jsonb and com_details jsonb. The values are copied at selection — the same invariant 00380 already froze for prices. Option IDs are retained for drift detection only; they are never re-read to render.

What the snapshot does

Structured selection auto-fills the free-text spec fields that ship today: finish, material, color_fabric, selected_dimensions, sku.

Free text stays the render source everywhere. Zero changes to shipped spec-book surfaces. If a designer overtypes the filled value, the linkage detaches and the line is flagged customized — her words win, and we stop pretending we know better.

We are not adding a tier. Options feed the tiers that already exist, which is why nothing downstream has to be rewritten.

Kits18

A sectional is one piece in the room.

It is five boxes on a truck and one thing on the schedule. The model should agree with the room, not with the truck.

Kit as one line

A kit Piece carries product_components. The configure step shows a component checklist — LAF chaise, armless, corner, RAF sofa, ottoman — with quantities and per-component options where the maker offers them.

One FF&E line. One price that rolls up. One PO block listing the components underneath it. The schedule reads "Fairmount Sectional," because that's what's going in the room.

Rejected

Exploding child FF&E lines. Five rows the designer has to keep in sync, five prices the client has to add up, and a schedule that reads like a packing list.

One library row per piece of the sectional. That is the duplicate problem from interview 02, deliberately manufactured — and the dedupe panel would spend its life trying to clean it up.

Phase 3 also opens the manufacturer portal to maker-declared options — currently a two-page scaffold whose onboarding copy already promises catalog product entry. It is the cleanest greenfield surface we have for authoring axes at the source instead of scraping them.

Out of scope19

What we're deliberately not building.

Architectures rejected
  • Shopify-style SKU matrix. A row per combination explodes combinatorially, and it is exactly the shape of the dead admin scaffold. It failed here once already; R70 deferred it with a rule we should keep: expose only columns that genuinely exist.
  • Child product rows per variant. Manufactures the duplicate problem at scale and puts us permanently at war with our own dedupe panel.
  • Variance through custom fields alone. A select-kind spec_field_defs would help one project. It is never reusable, and the library learns nothing.
  • Options only on the FF&E line. Cheapest path, and it forfeits the whitespace entirely — it is what Programa already does.
Escape hatches that stay, on purpose
  • Free text everywhere. Every spec field stays typeable. Structure fills it in; it never locks it.
  • Per-project custom columns. The right tool for the thing we didn't anticipate. It stays.
  • Allowance lines and the notes field. A designer who wants to keep working the way she works today can.
  • product_relations · alternative. Curated substitutes stay a curation act, not a computed one.

Scaffold disposition. Delete the Variant and CustomizationOption types and the admin variant editor — dead UI that silently discards input is worse than no UI. Stop writing finishes[], available_colors[], and pricing_tiers; backfill them lazily as candidates instead.

Roadmap20

Four phases. Each one is worth shipping alone.

Durations are rough scoping estimates, not commitments. Nothing here is built; Phase 1 is what we're asking to scope.

Phase 1 · ~4–5 weeks

Options on the master, spec on the PO

variance_class, the two option tables, the Piece-room options panel, the configure pane, selected_options snapshot, spec_snapshot on PO lines, spec block on the PDF.

Independent value: the PO payoff lands with zero axes authored — the snapshot carries the spec-book fields designers already fill by hand today.

Phase 2 · ~4 weeks

Capture, decisions, COM

Extension stops flattening; detected options land as candidates for human promotion; refresh-from-source diff; compare-across-an-axis in decisions; COM as a first-class axis with its own form and its own lead time.

Independent value: the library starts filling itself, and interview 01's top need is met.

Phase 3 · ~5 weeks

Kits and maker-declared options

product_components, sectional configuration, kit rollup pricing — plus catalog entry in the manufacturer portal so makers declare their own axes.

Independent value: sectionals become specifiable, and the manufacturer portal gets its first real job.

Phase 4 · open

The bespoke pipeline

Work orders, shop drawings, approval samples, the revision loop. Optionally, variant-to-SKU mapping for vendors who publish one.

Deliberately unscoped. It is a program, not a phase, and it should wait for real bespoke volume.

Sequencing logic

Phase 1 buys the vendor relationship. Phase 2 buys the data. Phase 3 buys the category. We measure axis adoption after Phase 2 and let that number decide whether Phase 3 happens on schedule or at all.

Risks21

Seven ways this goes wrong.

Each with the mitigation already designed into the proposal, not promised for later.

01 · Structure slows capture downThe thing interview 02 said would make her stop using it. Mitigation: nothing gates a save; a piece with no axes is complete; structure only ever auto-fills.
02 · Additive deltas are sometimes just wrongReal price lists aren't always base + delta. Mitigation: per-selection absolute price override, plus an explicit "price TBC" state rather than a confident wrong number.
03 · Snapshot driftThe vendor changes the finish list after we snapshotted. Mitigation: refresh-from-source diff extends the price-drift machinery that already exists on board pins — warn, never auto-mutate.
04 · Backfill corrupts the libraryScraped options are guesses with confidence scores. Mitigation: everything machine-extracted lands in product_option_candidates; a human promotes. No silent writes.
05 · We break the frozen invariantA selection writing back to products would poison every other project using that piece. Mitigation: selections never touch the master; enforce in review and in tests, the same way 00380 is enforced.
06 · We overbuild ahead of demandNobody authors an axis and we've shipped a configurator for ourselves. Mitigation: Phase 1 is minimal and its value doesn't depend on adoption; measure axis authoring before committing Phase 3.
07 · Trade deltas rotTwo price ladders per option is twice the maintenance and half the accuracy. Mitigation: price_trade_delta_cents is nullable and falls back to the retail delta. One number until a vendor gives us two.
The one we can't mitigate

This discovery is synthesized. Five composites are a hypothesis set, not a finding set. The cheapest possible correction is one session with Leah against these five archetypes before Phase 1 scoping closes.

The ask22

Open questions, then a green light.

Needs a ruling — Leah

The "Finish" taxonomy. A decision_type of "Finish" is already owed to Leah (§14.19). Does finish become an axis_kind, a decision type, or both — and do they share one vocabulary?

Related: does anyone own the axis names, or are they free-form? A house list ("Size", "Wood", "Finish", "Fabric Grade", "Base", "Leg") makes cross-library comparison possible and makes authoring slower.

Needs a ruling — commercial

Trade-delta policy. Do we ask vendors for a trade delta per option, or infer it from the master's trade ratio and let vendors correct us?

Also: when an option changes after the order is placed, does it route through the Back-of-House substitution exception — the one with the two-swatch review card that Leah has never actually seen?

Needs a decision — sequencing

When do we invest in Phase 3? Proposal: gate it on axis adoption measured 60 days after Phase 2, not on the calendar.

And: do makers declare options in the manufacturer portal (Phase 3), or do we stay designer-authored longer and keep the portal a scaffold?

The ask

Review this, then green-light Phase 1 scoping — options on the master, and a purchase order that carries the spec.

Nothing in this deck is built. No migration is written, no table exists, no code has changed. Phase 1 is the smallest thing that pays for itself the day it ships, whether or not a single axis ever gets authored.

Where this lands

The library already knows the chair. This is how it learns that the chair comes in walnut, in ninety-six inches, in the client's own linen — and how the maker finally reads that on the order.
