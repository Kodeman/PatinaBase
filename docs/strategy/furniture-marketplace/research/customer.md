# Patina maker commerce: customer evidence and buying hypotheses

Research date: October 13, 2026. Internal strategy research. All web sources below are primary company pages accessed on this date; undated program pages describe what their publishers currently advertise, not independently tested performance. No interviews were conducted. None of these companies is claimed as a Patina customer or interested prospect. **Fact** means documented evidence; **Inference** means interpretation; **Test assumption** means a proposed number or decision rule, not market data.

## Finding worth carrying into the deck

**Inference:** “Shopify for furniture” spans at least three different purchases: a first credible selling presence, a more efficient trade operation, and profitable new demand. The buyer, urgency and alternative differ. A maker with a waiting list may reject new leads. A mature manufacturer may reject another portal. A small workshop may buy assisted catalog preparation before software. Patina should identify a costly handoff it can improve and then decide whether storefronts, integrations or marketplace distribution make that improvement easier to buy.

The evidence supports testing a **repeatable-collection maker with controlled customization, an existing trade business and a named person handling quotes**. This is a proposed segment, not evidence of demand or sufficient market size. Larger companies below are useful operating benchmarks and counterexamples; they are not automatically reachable early customers.

## Seven real operating models

### 1. Eastvold: a small collection plus custom exceptions

**Fact:** Eastvold’s Classic Credenza Slim page lists a $3,948 price, designer/trade discounts, a 3–5 week lead time, free curbside delivery and a $200 white-glove upgrade. Dimension changes carry custom fees; rush work is by request. The item requires some assembly. These are terms for this product, not a companywide price or delivery guarantee. [Classic Credenza Slim](https://www.eastvoldfurniture.com/classic/p/classic-credenza-slim). Its own history identifies Amanda and Matt Eastvold as founders and welcomes customization of existing pieces. [About Eastvold](https://www.eastvoldfurniture.com/about-us).

**Inference:** The promising problem is converting “this piece, slightly different” into a complete, priced, accepted specification. Proposed economic buyer: owner/general manager; Matt or Amanda are research-entry names, not verified current software budget owners. The working user could be whoever answers quotes. Trigger: repeated custom inquiries or hiring the first sales administrator. Potential purchase: a branded collection, controlled options, sample tracking and quote preparation. Reasons to decline: already full capacity, low quote frequency, personal email sufficient, or custom judgment cannot be standardized. Ask to reconstruct the last dimension-change order, including the price decision and freight upgrade.

### 2. Loll Designs: made-to-order plus stocked exceptions

**Fact:** Loll offers trade pricing without minimums, account-manager support, online trade purchasing, 2D/3D files, specification sheets and customer’s-own-material ordering for soft seating/cushions. [Trade program](https://lolldesigns.com/pages/trade). Its FAQ distinguishes stocked items from made-to-order production; limits changes/cancellations to a two-business-day window before additional restrictions; describes oversized freight on pallets, curbside responsibilities and quoted premium services. Samples are available by request. Loll states its furniture is designed/manufactured in Duluth. [FAQ](https://lolldesigns.com/pages/faq).

**Inference:** Another product gallery offers little value. A potential wedge connects project requirements to product readiness, requested delivery services and the accepted order. Proposed buyer: trade-sales leader with operations/e-commerce approval; no personal budget owner verified. Trigger: growing contract work or repeated delivery-service mismatches. Payment hypothesis: integration or trade-operations subscription, subject to quantified reduction in manual handoffs. Refusal: established account managers and existing ordering already solve the problem. Test whether the outside designer’s project record adds useful context without replacing Loll’s authoritative data.

### 3. Blu Dot: sophisticated omnichannel trade service

**Fact:** Blu Dot advertises a 20% trade discount, no minimum, purchasing through a rep/online/in-store, visible trade prices and lead times, dedicated support, trade-only finishes and digital product resources. [Trade program](https://www.bludot.com/pages/trade). A separate swatch catalog includes free material swatches and paid rug samples; “all samples are free” would be wrong. [Swatches](https://www.bludot.com/collections/swatches).

**Inference:** This is a high competitive baseline, not a business lacking an online presence. Proposed buyer: trade/contract leadership; e-commerce and revenue operations would influence any integration. Trigger: fragmented project context across purchasing channels or a measurable need for new qualified studios. Payment hypothesis: attributed qualified demand or an integration with proven conversion/service value. Refusal: existing rep relationships, duplicate data and Patina’s unproven audience. The research question is whether information about a designer’s accepted specification improves the existing channel—not whether Blu Dot needs a website.

### 4. Stickley: a portal still leaves a human order handoff

**Fact:** Stickley’s corporate trade FAQ says POs are emailed to a concierge. It accepts full payment at order or a 50% deposit with the balance before shipment. This trade channel ships to approved warehouses/commercial receiving locations, not residences; freight depends on destination. Its dealer/designer portal provides price lists, inventory programs and acknowledgments. It offers free memo samples and separately sells sample sets. [Corporate trade FAQ](https://www.stickley.com/pages/to-the-trade-faq). These are corporate trade terms, not every Stickley retail channel’s terms.

**Inference:** “Has a portal” does not mean “automated specification-to-order.” Proposed economic buyer: head of trade sales, with order-management/IT sponsorship. User: concierge or order-entry specialist. Trigger: email order backlog, failed receiver information or expansion of the trade program. Potential purchase: structured PO intake and acknowledgments that preserve the concierge relationship. Refusal: integration burden, existing software roadmap, or exceptions too rare to justify change. Ask which fields most often force the concierge to return a PO and how many touches occur before acceptance.

### 5. Bernhardt: trade self-service is already offered

**Fact:** Bernhardt’s trade program advertises no order minimum, trade pricing, real-time inventory, wishlists and online ordering. It supports COM fabric/leather orders and a specialized service team. Professional designers and furnishing retailers qualify using a business license or resale certificate; the application proceeds through review and account setup. [Trade program](https://www.bernhardt.com/trade-program/).

**Inference:** Trade verification, private pricing and ordering are table stakes for some manufacturers. Proposed buyer: trade-channel leadership plus digital commerce; budget ownership remains unverified. Trigger: measurable loss between specification and purchase, or new dealer/designer distribution goals. A possible offer is externally initiated project specifications that enter existing systems accurately. Refusal: existing features, lead attribution disputes, dealer conflict or no material improvement. Require a concrete comparison with current workflows before treating this segment as a sales opportunity.

### 6. Room & Board: service is bundled into the furniture channel

**Fact:** Room & Board is a retailer/manufacturing-partner channel benchmark, not an independent workshop. Its free business program provides project coordinators, payment terms, COM customization and delivery services. The published 10% business discount begins after $50,000 spending; additional personalized delivery services carry a 3% fee. [Business program](https://www.roomandboard.com/business). Its consumer custom offering permits changes within existing collections, advertises custom sizing and samples, and describes free cancellation/90-day returns; these terms must not be generalized to other makers or channels. [Made for You](https://www.roomandboard.com/catalog/made-for-you).

**Inference:** Designers compare Patina against human service bundled into product margin. Proposed buyer for a partnership: business-channel leadership; likely not an early standalone SaaS account. Trigger: a strategic distribution or project-data partnership. Refusal: its coordinated commerce and fulfillment may already own the customer experience. Patina must prove why another intermediary improves that experience. This example also warns against imposing one marketplace return policy on heterogeneous makers.

### 7. OFS: specification infrastructure is deep in contract furniture

**Fact:** OFS expresses lead times as delivery-week estimates, dependent on capacity and material availability and confirmed at order submission. Large projects and textile/material choices can extend them. [Lead times](https://ofs.com/lead-times). Its site promotes a COM calculator, textile approvals, memo samples and manufacturer resources. [About and resources](https://ofs.com/about).

**Inference:** Contract manufacturers require an integration-led product, not a simple custom-options form. Proposed buyer: sales operations or digital-product leadership; dealers and IT become stakeholders. Trigger: poor transfer from external design specifications into quoting. Potential purchase: accurate field mapping, project handoff and change history. Refusal: established specification tools, dealer economics and enterprise procurement. Defer an OFS-scale deployment until Patina can demonstrate interoperability on a narrower collection. The lesson is that delivery semantics—ship date versus delivery date—must be explicit even in the small-maker pilot.

## Customer segmentation and the actual budget conversation

These are **hypotheses to recruit against**, not findings from interviews:

| Segment | Trigger worth testing | Economic buyer / working user | Plausible paid outcome | Disqualifier |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Solo custom craft | First repeat collection or wholesale opportunity | Owner / owner | Assisted collection and inquiry qualification | Every piece starts from scratch; no available capacity |
| Trade-ready collection maker | Quotes consume scarce selling/production time | Owner or GM / sales administrator | Fewer corrections; faster accepted quotes | Too few repeatable orders to amortize setup |
| Growing multi-channel brand | New rep, dealer or trade team | Sales director + operations / account managers | Shared catalog and protected attribution | Needs demand only; refuses workflow adoption |
| Established manufacturer | Integration project or measurable channel leakage | Trade leader + IT / order-entry staff | Interoperability and reduced reconciliation | Procurement cost exceeds reachable contract value |
| Retailer/network operator | Strategic assortment or new project channel | Channel leader / coordinators | Qualified demand and orchestration | Already controls equivalent service end to end |

Do not ask only “Would you pay?” Ask which existing budget could fund the purchase, whose approval is required, which expense/work would disappear, and what happens if the pilot stops. A compliment from a showroom associate is not budget-owner validation. A signed low-price pilot can still be a favor rather than repeatable demand; renewal after real usage is stronger evidence.

## Economic reality: manufacturer margin is not Patina’s fee allowance

**Fact, scale context:** Hooker’s FY2026 release, published April 16, 2026, covers 52 weeks ended February 1, 2026. Continuing operations recorded $278.139m sales, 26.4% gross margin and a 5.9% operating loss margin, including substantial impairment charges. Domestic Upholstery recorded $111.177m sales and 18.3% gross margin; its $16.897m operating loss included $15.0m noncash impairments. [Company results, Table V and commentary](https://investors.hookerfurnishings.com/news-releases/news-release-details/hooker-furnishings-reports-fiscal-2026-fourth-quarter-net-income).

**Fact, different business mix:** MillerKnoll’s June 24, 2026 release covers 52 weeks ended May 30, 2026: $3.8417bn consolidated revenue, 38.8% gross margin and 5.2% GAAP operating margin. Its Global Retail segment reported 44.5% gross margin and 2.3% operating margin on $1.1065bn revenue. [Company FY2026 results and segment tables](https://news.millerknoll.com/2026-06-24-MillerKnoll%2C-Inc-Reports-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Fiscal-Year-2026-Results).

**Interpretation:** These reported margins are neither an independent-maker benchmark nor incremental contribution, cash flow or willingness to pay. They mix different products, channels and accounting costs. They demonstrate why a headline gross margin cannot justify a marketplace commission. Obtain a participating maker’s order-level material, labor, packaging, freight, payment, warranty and selling costs. Separate genuinely incremental orders from orders diverted from existing channels, and price a fee against the counterfactual route the maker would otherwise use.

## Test assumptions: a buyer-value model, not a forecast

For an assisted specification tool, use:

`monthly customer value = genuinely avoidable admin cost + expected avoided error cost + incremental order contribution − new upkeep − software fee`.

**Illustration only:** at a $149 monthly fee and $45/hour labor value, labor alone requires 3.31 hours/month of benefit before new upkeep. If 20 monthly quotes save 15 minutes each, gross time value is $225. Subtract one hour updating the catalog ($45), then the fee: $31 remaining. If only ten quotes occur, the same model yields a negative $81.50. Saved owner time is capacity/opportunity value unless it actually reduces paid cost; do not present it as cash savings.

Add implementation separately. **Test assumption:** eight maker-hours to establish options and assets valued at $45/hour is $360 of customer effort. A $500 setup fee makes initial burden $860. At $31 monthly net benefit, simple payback is almost 28 months—an unattractive case. Assisted onboarding only works economically if it reduces that burden, raises recurring value, or replaces an expense the maker would otherwise incur. All figures here are deliberately falsifiable inputs; no source establishes these prices, wages or savings.

For new-demand pricing, estimate realized contribution on the incremental order after production and fulfillment, then subtract acquisition/commission cost and customer-service burden. Do not count a requested quote as merchandise volume, a deposit as full revenue, or existing customers as newly acquired demand. Model capacity displacement: an additional order is not incremental value if it replaces a more profitable job.

## Interviews that reveal a purchase rather than gather opinions

1. Show the last three actual trade inquiries. Which became orders, and why did the others stop? Capture dates and documents with permission.
2. Which missing field caused the last returned quote or PO? Could software have detected it before a human evaluated craft feasibility?
3. Who sets custom pricing, promises timing and accepts the final configuration? Where is their approval recorded?
4. Which five product families have stable option rules? Which exception must always remain human-approved?
5. How are sample approval, COM receipt and material availability connected to production release today?
6. Show an order that changed after deposit. Who absorbed redesign, material or freight cost?
7. Which channel actually brought the last new designer? Did a rep, showroom or existing relationship expect commission?
8. Which product facts may Patina publish, which remain private, and who updates each fact when it changes?
9. What is the fully loaded cost of a damaged or failed delivery, including staff time and redelivery? Who pays today?
10. Would you want more orders this month? If not, what kind of better order would you accept instead?
11. Which paid tool or service would this replace? Can the budget owner approve a defined paid pilot now?
12. After the pilot, can the maker complete the next quote without Patina staff assembling it? Can the designer reuse it without entering it again?

## Patina-specific bounds and proposed decision

**Repository facts:** `docs/vision/VISION.md` centers the studio, with marketplace sales through designer-led projects. `docs/vision/VISION-DECISIONS.md` V14 assigns Patina’s maker-lane margin to the maker trade discount while preserving studio markup. Neither supplies permission to stack a new seller commission on top. `marketing/canon/claims.md` does not substantiate marketplace traction or time savings. Direct code inspection found manufacturer onboarding displays “coming soon.”

Reusable code foundations include `packages/supabase/src/hooks/use-layer-products.ts`, `use-proposals.ts`, `use-procurement.ts`, and `packages/catalog-ui/src/product-detail/maker-story.tsx`. These establish implementation surface, not production completeness, live integration or merchant readiness. New maker tenancy, publishing control, accepted configuration, account pricing, source-of-truth synchronization and commercial responsibility need explicit validation.

**Recommendation, conditional:** recruit around a trade-ready collection maker’s actual quote rather than a generic marketplace invitation. Run a small paid “collection-to-accepted-quote” pilot with maker-held payments and existing relationships first. Record baseline and intervention time for maker, designer and Patina separately; capture maintenance, exceptions and rejected configurations. Continue only when a budget owner renews for the recurring workflow, designers avoid duplicate work, and Patina’s service effort declines across repeat orders. If makers consistently value introductions but not workflow, test a distribution business honestly; do not hide it inside a SaaS forecast.
