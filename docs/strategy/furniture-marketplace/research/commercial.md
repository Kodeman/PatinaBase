# Patina furniture commerce: commercial design and red team

Prepared October 13, 2026. All Patina prices, conversion rates, margins, customer counts, and forecasts below are explicit hypotheses for testing. They are not observed traction or validated willingness to pay. Dollar figures are USD. Examples are fictional. This analysis recommends a narrow supplier extension to a studio-first product; it does not establish that Patina has enough active designer demand to support a marketplace.

## Decision to test

Start with trade-ready small manufacturers selling configurable, repeatable furniture to design studios. Sell a reduction in the work and mistakes between specification and accepted order. A supplier should obtain value from its existing designer relationships before Patina promises new customers. A public marketplace becomes an option after repeat purchasing and matching are demonstrated.

The economic distinction matters: a storefront gives a seller a place to transact; a sales channel must produce qualified buyers; a workflow product must remove measurable work. Combining these promises obscures which one customers actually buy.

## Three narrowly defined customers

## What the alternatives establish

Shopify lists Basic/Grow/Advanced at $39/$105/$399 monthly, with lower annual-billing equivalents. This anchors the inexpensive storefront alternative; Patina cannot treat an attractive site as a premium economic moat. [Shopify pricing](https://www.shopify.com/pricing)

Shopify already supports up to three active B2B catalogs across markets on Basic, Grow, and Advanced; Plus adds unlimited catalogs and direct company/location assignment. “Trade pricing” alone is not an unserved category. [Shopify B2B documentation](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/b2b/catalogs/index)

Faire charges North American brands 15% marketplace commission plus $10 on a new customer's first order; merchant-brought Faire Direct orders have 0% commission, with payment processing separate. This supports distinguishing supplied demand from existing relationships, not copying its rate into furniture. [Faire fees](https://www.faire.com/support/articles/360015893392?section=2)

Etsy's transaction fee is 6.5%, including charged shipping and gift wrapping, with other possible fees separate. Avoid pitching “commission savings” unless Patina can replace the actual acquisition channel without losing its demand. [Etsy fees policy](https://www.etsy.com/legal/fees/)

Stripe publishes standard domestic-card pricing of 2.9% plus $0.30 in its US pricing result. Payment expense is real and separate from a platform's margin. Model deposits and balances as separate transactions when applicable. [Stripe pricing](https://stripe.com/pricing?rel=canonical)

Connect's commercial model varies with who controls pricing: Stripe-billed connected accounts and platform-controlled pricing have different platform obligations and fees. No assumed payments revenue share belongs in Patina's base case without an actual commercial agreement. [Stripe Connect pricing](https://stripe.com/connect/pricing)

These are product/price facts from primary sources, not evidence that Patina can acquire customers at the assumed prices.

## Three concrete offers worth charging for

**A. Maker commission desk: $99–199/month; $400–900 setup.** One branded collection of up to ten product families, structured inquiry, human-confirmed quote, versioned approval, and order handoff. No promise of instant custom pricing or free ongoing merchandising. At $149, four hours actually recovered at an assumed $40/hour produce $160 of capacity value, barely clearing the recurring price before setup. Thus price cannot be justified by vague “time savings”; either the hours are larger, demand is incremental, or the offer is too expensive. Patina delivery hypothesis: 6–10 setup hours at $60 fully loaded cost = $360–600; 0.5 support hour plus $15 tools monthly = $45 before subscription collection. A $99 plan with two support hours loses contribution. Start with a capped paid pilot.

**B. Studio-connected trade desk: $299–599/month; $750–2,000 setup.** One collection, roughly 30–100 product families, private trade terms, structured quote requests, approved configurations, and designer-project handoff. This is the recommended offer. Fifteen hours recovered at $45/hour represent $675/month of capacity; a $349 fee leaves a possible value surplus, but it becomes cash savings only if overtime/contractor spending actually falls. Patina hypothesis: 8–20 setup hours at $60 = $480–1,200; 1–2 monthly support hours plus $30 tools = $90–150. Charge separately for data repair outside scope. Use a $500–1,000 paid pilot credited toward setup, then require renewal at the disclosed recurring price.

**C. Attributed new-business channel: 3–6% of fulfilled merchandise revenue on agreed net-new relationships; 0% acquisition commission on merchant-brought relationships.** Offer this only to the trade-ready cohort with capacity. Example: $3,500 order at 30% pre-Patina contribution produces $1,050; a 5% fee consumes $175, leaving $875. If contribution is 10%, the same fee consumes half of it. Patina's illustrative per-order cost is $40 coordination, $5 tools, and a 0.5% merchandise-value reserve ($17.50), leaving $112.50 before buyer acquisition and fixed costs. Three hours of intervention at $60/hour destroys that margin. Processing is separately paid by the merchant in this hypothesis. Publish attribution and repeat-order rules; do not take permanent credit for relationships Patina did not originate.

Do not launch enterprise packaging yet. A paid, bounded integration discovery may qualify an established manufacturer, but custom ERP commitments must not set the core roadmap. These ranges are bounded by value and delivery-cost arithmetic, not buyer research.

## Seller ROI: contribution, not GMV

Use three separate ledgers, with a clear counterfactual:

1. **Time/capacity ledger:** minutes removed per existing quote/order × actual event count × actual loaded hourly cost. Report hours and theoretical capacity value separately from cash. Salaried time does not automatically become savings.
2. **Avoided-cost ledger:** verified canceled software/contractor/overtime expense plus evidenced avoided rework, net of replacement costs. Never count the same coordinator hours here and in time savings. Rework already embedded in order contribution must not be counted again.
3. **Incremental-order ledger:** fulfilled orders that would otherwise not have occurred × contribution after materials, incremental production labor, freight subsidy, payment expense, expected damage/returns, commissions, and incremental customer service. Subtract displaced existing orders and the contribution they would have earned.

Annual net cash benefit = incremental-order contribution + realized avoided cash costs − subscriptions − setup − seller implementation labor − channel fees not already deducted. Cash ROI = net cash benefit / total incremental seller cash outlay. Report a separate capacity-adjusted case, with no claim of cash payback.

**Illustrative small-manufacturer case to validate:** 25 quote requests/month; 36 minutes saved each = 15 hours; $45/hour = $675 theoretical capacity. Only four hours reduce paid overtime = $180 actual cash savings; the remaining 11 hours are operational capacity, excluded from cash ROI. One genuinely additional $3,500 fulfilled order/month, 30% contribution before the 5% Patina acquisition fee, contributes $875 afterward. Assume $60/month of a canceled tool. Benefits = $1,115/month. Against $349/month plus $900 setup and 12 seller implementation hours at $45 ($540), first-year net cash benefit = $7,752; first-year outlay = $5,628; cash ROI ≈138%. The unchanged-demand case yields negative $2,748 in year one. The 11 recovered hours must not also be monetized as labor savings if they enable that additional order.

Stress-test contribution at 15/30/40%, incremental fulfilled orders at 0/0.5/1 monthly, and overtime reduction at 0/4 hours. Validate invoices and production capacity before presenting the favorable case. Two deposits are not two incremental orders; a refund reverses contribution, not merely dashboard GMV.

## Acquisition and the demand bottleneck

Seller funnel: named suitable business → economic-buyer conversation → qualified workflow → scoped pilot → paid activation → retained use. An illustrative 100 contacts ×25% qualified ×40% pilot ×60% paid ×80% activated yields 4.8 active accounts. At $12/contact, $60/qualification, and $150/pilot, cost is $4,200, or $875 per activation before setup. Include founder selling time, failed pilots, travel, and data cleanup. Paying but never activating is not success.

A manufacturer joining does not create designer demand. Count designers with active eligible projects, not signups or monthly logins. Demand example: 100 active studios ×4 projects/year ×3 eligible furniture order opportunities ×60% supply match ×50% quote progression ×40% order conversion ×95% fulfillment = approximately 137 fulfilled orders/year. At $3,500, that is approximately $479,000 merchandise value. If only 60% qualifies as Patina-originated, a 5% fee yields roughly $14,000 revenue before costs. That cannot finance a broad software business by itself. Keep each order opportunity unique; do not count three competing quotes as three orders.

## Three-year model: explicit illustrative outputs

Use monthly seller cohorts. New paid activations arrive after the funnel's lag; existing sellers churn monthly. Bill activated accounts only. Separately cohort active studios with 2% monthly churn and budget their acquisition and support. In this model, studio “additions” are already activated studios, not leads. Each studio uses the demand formula above; fulfilled orders are capped by available sellers ×2 additional orders/month, then 60% qualify for referral fees. This supply cap is illustrative and must become category- and capacity-specific.

The base case consumes roughly $458k cumulatively across three years and remains annually loss-making in year three. Only about $32k of its year-three revenue is acquisition fees; subscriptions carry the model. At the stated year-three spending, roughly 179 average paid sellers are needed versus approximately 169 modeled. Ending with 220 sellers does not establish full-year break-even. Upside assumes simultaneous improvements in price, conversion, retention, and service effort; treat it as a demanding success condition, not a forecast. Add correlated downside: harder onboarding also delays activation and increases churn.

A separate cash sheet must track invoice collections, prepaid annual subscriptions, setup collections, payroll, and reserves. Merchandise deposits owed to sellers are liabilities, never operating revenue or runway.

## Build boundaries and gates

Build the shared product specification, private terms, quote, approval, and studio-project handoff first. Preserve the maker's brand and existing website. Use assisted imports and exports before deep ERP integrations. Give designers a reason to use it even when no marketplace transaction happens.

Do not expand into a general website builder, broad DTC acquisition, inventory ownership, financing, fulfillment guarantees, physical showrooms, manufacturing ERP, or unrestricted bespoke commissions during this test. These businesses require separate economics. The purpose is to strengthen the studio workflow, not create several unrelated operating companies.

Proposed gates, to agree before spending: first 20 qualified interviews must produce at least five paid scoped pilots; by ten completed pilots, median activation ≤30 days and median setup ≤10 hours; at least seven renew at the disclosed price and show repeat live workflow use; fully loaded seller CAC payback ≤12 months using recurring contribution; support ≤1 hour/account/month for the $349 offer. Assess retention at 90 and 180 days because furniture cycles are long. Stop marketplace expansion if buyers are mostly pre-existing relationships, supply cannot answer reliably, or fulfilled incremental contribution fails to justify combined seller fees and Patina service cost. Small samples justify another bounded experiment, not scaling certainty.

## Ten interview questions

1. Show the last completed order from inquiry through final payment. Where did information get re-entered, and by whom?
2. Pull the last ten quotes: which converted, how long did each take, and why did the others stop?
3. Show the last incorrect dimension, finish, or delivery assumption. What was the actual remediation bill and unrecovered labor?
4. On the last $3,000–$5,000 order, what remained after materials, production labor, commissions, freight subsidy, payment costs, and service?
5. What software, contractor, rep, or advertising invoices did you pay last month to obtain and serve these orders?
6. What happened the last time demand exceeded production capacity? Which work was displaced or declined?
7. Identify the last genuinely new designer customer. How did they discover you, and what did acquiring them cost?
8. Who owns the account when a designer, rep, showroom, and website all touch the sale? Show the current agreement or dispute.
9. What was your most recent software purchase and cancellation? Who approved each, what evidence mattered, and what implementation work was required?
10. For the next live collection, will the economic buyer sign a bounded paid pilot at the written price, name the catalog owner, and provide baseline records? Record payment, refusal reason, and required conditions; enthusiasm is not purchase evidence.
